Polynomial commitments

Question. ZeroTest assumes a locked oracle holding fixed polynomials. How can we make the verifier's later evaluation queries trustworthy in an ordinary cryptographic protocol?

A polynomial commitment scheme commits to a polynomial \(f\in\mathbb F[X]\) of degree at most \(d\) and allows short proofs of evaluations \(f(z)=y\). It supplies the same read-only behavior as the abstract polynomial oracle, but under computational assumptions.

AlgorithmInput and outputPurpose
Setup\(\mathrm{Setup}(d,\lambda)\to pp\)Prepare public parameters \(pp\) for the degree bound \(d\) and security parameter \(\lambda\).
Commit\(\mathrm{Commit}(pp,f)\to c\)Bind a short commitment \(c\) to the polynomial.
Open\(\mathrm{Open}(pp,f,z)\to\pi\)Produce an opening proof \(\pi\) for the point \(z\) and true value \(f(z)\).
Verify\(\mathrm{Verify}(pp,c,z,y,\pi)\to\{0,1\}\)Check the claimed value \(y\) against the commitment.

There are two formal properties in the course:

Correctness and evaluation binding

Correctness requires the verifier always to accept an honest opening of the polynomial that was committed: \(\mathrm{Verify}(pp,c,z,f(z),\pi)=1\) for the correctly generated \(c,\pi\).

Evaluation binding forbids a polynomial-time adversary from finding the same \(c\) and \(z\), two different values \(y\ne y'\), and successful openings for both values. More precisely, the probability of producing

\[\mathrm{Verify}(pp,c,z,y,\pi)=\mathrm{Verify}(pp,c,z,y',\pi')=1,\qquad y\ne y'\]

must be negligible in \(\lambda\). “Negligible” means smaller than \(1/p(\lambda)\) for every positive polynomial \(p\), for all sufficiently large \(\lambda\).

Correctness makes true oracle answers usable, and evaluation binding prevents inconsistent responses at the same queried point. These are precisely the two operations the verifier relied upon in the ideal locked box. \(\square\)

A sound polynomial IOP also relies on its polynomials obeying declared degree bounds and on access to a consistent committed polynomial—not merely on two openings to a single point being inconsistent. A concrete commitment instantiation must provide the required degree enforcement and evaluation soundness. The lecture defines the interface but does not prove a concrete construction such as KZG.

Not guaranteed: neither hiding the polynomial nor zero-knowledge follows from the two listed properties. This is why the lecture first constructs a SNARG, leaving zero-knowledge randomization outside the covered details.

Why this is useful. The verifier can now request \(f(r)\) and \(q(r)\) for ZeroTest without receiving the coefficients of \(f,q\). We still have an interactive exchange because the verifier must supply the unpredictable point \(r\).

Schnorr's protocol, derived from its goal

Question. Before replacing an interactive challenge with a hash, what does the challenge actually protect against, and why does the verifier's equation work?

Let \(G\) be a cyclic group with prime order \(q\), and let \(g\) generate it. “Order \(q\)” means \(g^q=1_G\), and exponents are computed modulo \(q\). The public statement is \(h=g^x\); the prover knows the secret exponent \(x\in\mathbb Z_q\). The verifier wants evidence of this knowledge without learning \(x\).

The prover samples a fresh random exponent \(r\in\mathbb Z_q\) and sends \(u=g^r\). The verifier independently samples a challenge \(c\in\mathbb Z_q\). The prover replies with \(z=r+cx\bmod q\). The verifier checks \(g^z=u\,h^c\).

Prover \(P\)
⟶
Verifier \(V\)
Choose \(r\), send \(u=g^r\)
⟶
Receive \(u\)
Wait for unknown challenge
⟵
Random \(c\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q\)
Send \(z=r+cx\)
⟶
Check \(g^z=u h^c\)
Verify the algebra in an actual finite group

For transparent arithmetic, take the subgroup of order 11 generated by 2 modulo 23, with witness \(x=3\) and public \(h=8\).

HW2 · Problem 3(a), 3(b), 3(c)

Completeness, zero knowledge, and extraction

Part (a) proves every honest response passes. Part (b) constructs an honest-verifier zero-knowledge (HVZK) simulator without \(x\). Part (c) extracts \(x\) from two accepting responses to different challenges for the same \(u\).

See the three complete solutions

(a) Perfect completeness

\[g^z=g^{r+cx}=g^r(g^x)^c=u h^c.\]

The first equality substitutes the honest response. The second uses the exponent rule \(g^{a+b}=g^ag^b\); the last uses definitions of \(u\) and \(h\).

Thus every honest prover response satisfies the verifier's exact acceptance equation. This proves perfect completeness. \(\square\)

(b) Honest-verifier zero knowledge

The simulator must produce a transcript \((u,c,z)\) with the same distribution as an honest interaction, using only public \(h\). It samples \(c,z\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q\) uniformly and sets \(u=g^z h^{-c}\). By construction \(g^z=u h^c\), so verification passes.

In a real conversation, \(r,c\) are independent uniform scalars and \(z=r+cx\). For any fixed \(c\), adding the fixed value \(cx\) to uniform \(r\) preserves uniformity of \(z\), so the real pair \((c,z)\) is uniform. Once \(c,z,h\) are fixed, the verifier equation uniquely determines \(u=g^z h^{-c}\). That is precisely the simulator's construction.

The real and simulated triples therefore have the same distribution, even though the simulator never knew \(x\). This proves HVZK for the stated honest verifier. \(\square\)

(c) Extract the witness

Suppose the prover produces two accepting transcripts with the same first message \(u\), but distinct challenges \(c\ne c'\) and responses \(z,z'\). From verification we know \(g^z=u h^c\) and \(g^{z'}=u h^{c'}\). Divide these group equalities to eliminate the shared \(u\):

\[g^{z-z'}=h^{c-c'}=(g^x)^{c-c'}=g^{x(c-c')}.\]

Since \(g\) has order \(q\), equality of its powers means the exponents agree modulo \(q\). Because \(q\) is prime and \(c-c'\ne0\), the difference has an inverse. Therefore

\[\boxed{x=(z-z')(c-c')^{-1}\pmod q.}\]

An extractor obtains the two transcripts by rewinding the prover to the state after sending \(u\) and issuing a fresh challenge. If two independent challenges accidentally coincide, which occurs with probability \(1/q\), it cannot apply this formula to that pair.

Two valid answers to different challenges force one recoverable witness \(x\). This is the special-soundness fact that later bounds how many challenges a false statement can answer. \(\square\)

Sequential and parallel repetition

Question. Repeating a one-bit challenge makes cheating unlikely. Why can the same number of tests behave differently for zero-knowledge simulation?

A three-message Sigma protocol has the order first message \(u\) → verifier challenge \(c\) → response \(z\). When repeated \(t\) times sequentially, the messages have order \(u_1,c_1,z_1,u_2,c_2,z_2,\ldots,u_t,c_t,z_t\). In a parallel repetition, the prover sends all \(u_1,\ldots,u_t\) before receiving the challenge vector \((c_1,\ldots,c_t)\), then sends all responses together.

Sequential

\(u_1\to c_1\to z_1\)
\(u_2\to c_2\to z_2\)
\(\cdots\)
\(u_t\to c_t\to z_t\)

Each challenge arrives before the next commitment is constructed.

Parallel

\((u_1,\ldots,u_t)\)
↓
\((c_1,\ldots,c_t)\)
↓
\((z_1,\ldots,z_t)\)

All commitments are fixed before any challenge is revealed.

HW2 · Problem 2(a–c) · Hamiltonian-cycle ZK

The original Hamiltonian-cycle protocol has perfect completeness and soundness error \(1/2\). Repeating it \(t\) times, the verifier accepts iff every instance accepts. (a) Explain why sequential repetition remains ZK. (b) Explain why the standard simulator does not prove ZK for arbitrary malicious verifiers under parallel repetition. (c) Prove honest-verifier ZK under parallel repetition.

See the protocol-by-protocol explanation

(a) Sequential ZK. Run the original simulator for each session while treating the adversarial verifier, including its current state and previous transcript, as the verifier for that session. The first simulated session finishes before the next session begins. Thus the simulator can generate each session's transcript before interacting with the verifier's next-stage behavior. A sequence of at most polynomially many indistinguishable replacements is still indistinguishable by a hybrid argument. The continuation state must be respected; it is not enough to assert that a malicious verifier's sessions are probabilistically independent.

(b) Parallel difficulty. The standard Hamiltonian simulator prepares commitments suitable for one of two potential challenge types and guesses which one the verifier will request. With \(t\) parallel instances, it must prepare all \(t\) first messages before seeing the vector of challenges. Guessing a particular vector has probability \(2^{-t}\) when the challenges are uniformly independent; the expected number of full guesses is \(2^t\). A malicious verifier can also correlate its challenge choices with all the first messages. Therefore the standard sequential rewinding argument does not establish general parallel ZK. This is a limitation of that proof technique, not a theorem that all parallel protocols are non-ZK.

(c) Parallel HVZK. Against an honest verifier, the \(t\) challenges are independent uniform bits, independent of the first messages. Run the original honest-verifier simulator independently \(t\) times. Each simulated triple has the honest distribution, and their product distribution is exactly the distribution of \(t\) independent honest executions. Package the triples into the three batched protocol messages.

Sequential composition can simulate a completed session before the next begins. Parallel HVZK works because honest challenges are independent. The ordinary parallel simulation proof for a malicious verifier fails because it must prepare every first message before seeing the joint challenge vector. These conclusions follow from the message order, not from the word “parallel” alone. \(\square\)

The distinction also matters in SNARG construction: batching many first messages retains a three-message structure, allowing one Fiat–Shamir hash to replace an entire challenge vector.

Fiat–Shamir: removing interaction

Question. How can everyone reconstruct the verifier's unpredictable challenge without contacting the verifier?

In a Sigma-style protocol, the prover first commits to a first message \(u\), the verifier sends a uniformly random challenge \(c\), and the prover computes the response \(z\). Fiat–Shamir replaces the transmitted challenge with the value of a hash on the public statement and the already-fixed first message:

\[c=H(\operatorname{encode}(x,u))\in\mathcal C.\]

Here, \(x\) is the public statement, \(\mathcal C\) is the challenge space, and \(H\) is modeled as a random oracle that returns values in that space. The encoding must be unambiguous and should bind protocol and public parameters in a real implementation.

The prover calculates \(u\), then \(c\), then \(z\), and sends \(\pi=(u,z)\). The verifier recomputes \(c\) from \(x,u\) and runs the original verification equation. An observer needs only the public statement and proof string.

Why this reproduces honest verification

An honest prover produces \(u\) in the same way as before, derives \(c\) using the agreed hash input, and computes the original response \(z\). The verifier recomputes exactly that \(c\), then applies exactly the same original acceptance predicate. Hence any original algebraic completeness identity still holds.

Non-interactivity succeeds because both sides compute the same challenge after the prover's first message is fixed; completeness survives because the verifier's final check is unchanged. This reasoning alone does not establish the required soundness or zero-knowledge theorem. \(\square\)

Security precondition. The hash must be modeled appropriately, and the original challenge space must already make cheating unlikely. Simply hashing a one-bit challenge does not turn constant soundness error into negligible error.

Homework 3: non-interactive Schnorr

HW3 · Problem 2(a) · Original task

Let \(G\) be a cyclic group of prime order \(q\) with generator \(g\), public \(h=g^x\), and secret witness \(x\). Apply Fiat–Shamir to Schnorr's identification protocol. Specify the one-message proof \(\pi\).

Show the complete construction

Choose fresh \(r\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q\) and compute \(u=g^r\). Then derive \(c=H(h,u)\in\mathbb Z_q\) from the public value and first message. Set \(z=r+cx\bmod q\).

\[\boxed{\pi=(u,z),\quad u=g^r,\quad c=H(h,u),\quad z=r+cx\pmod q.}\]

There is no need to include \(c\) in \(\pi\): the verifier can calculate it using \(h\) and \(u\). The witness \(x\) is never sent.

The first message is fixed before the hash-derived challenge, and the response uses that same challenge. The transmitted proof contains exactly the data the verifier cannot calculate locally. \(\square\)

HW3 · Problem 2(b) · Original task

Write the verification algorithm for \(\pi=(u,z)\), and explain the equation that honest proofs satisfy.

Show verifier and correctness proof

Validate the public group parameters and the inputs \(h,u,z\). Recompute \(c=H(h,u)\in\mathbb Z_q\). Accept precisely when \(g^z=u h^c\) holds in \(G\).

For an honest proof, \(g^z=g^{r+cx}=g^r(g^x)^c=u h^c\), so verification always accepts.

The equation succeeds because the prover's response encodes the relation \(h=g^x\) using the same challenge that the verifier independently reconstructs. This establishes completeness, not soundness or zero knowledge by itself. \(\square\)

Homework 3: guessing and transferability

HW3 · Problem 2(c) · Original task

A cheating prover fixes response \(z\) and first message \(u\) before asking the random oracle for \(H(h,u)\). Assume \(h\ne1_G\). Show why \(Q\) tested candidates have success probability at most \(Q/q\).

Derive the bound rather than quoting it

The verifier accepts only if \(g^z=u h^c\). Once \(z,u\) are fixed, rearrange this as \(h^c=g^zu^{-1}\), which is one fixed group value. In a prime-order cyclic group, every nonidentity element \(h\) generates the entire group. Therefore the map \(c\mapsto h^c\) is injective over \(\mathbb Z_q\): at most one challenge \(c\) satisfies the equation.

For a fresh random-oracle input, the challenge is uniformly distributed over \(q\) values. Thus a fixed trial succeeds with probability at most \(1/q\). By the union bound, the probability that at least one of \(Q\) trials succeeds is at most \(\sum_{i=1}^Q1/q=Q/q\). Repeating the same hash query does not create a new trial because a random oracle returns its stored answer.

The bound follows from a unique successful challenge for each fixed pair \((u,z)\), not from claiming that a hash function is vaguely unpredictable. This argument covers the specified guessing strategy, not arbitrary adversarial soundness or knowledge extraction. \(\square\)

HW3 · Problem 2(d) · Original task

Can a publicly verifiable non-interactive zero-knowledge proof convince a second verifier? Why does the recording of an interactive zero-knowledge conversation not provide the same evidence?

Show the distinction

A publicly verifiable NIZK proof string \(\pi\) can be copied. A second verifier who knows the same statement and public setup can run the prescribed verification algorithm on it. Its acceptance does not require direct interaction with the original prover.

An interactive zero-knowledge transcript is different: a simulator can generate an indistinguishable accepting-looking conversation without the witness (and without a real prover). Therefore the first verifier's recorded view alone is not convincing evidence to a third party that the real prover participated.

The non-interactive proof is transferable because anyone can verify its prescribed public relation; an interactive transcript is deniable because its distribution can be simulated. Neither statement rules out all live-relay attacks. \(\square\)

Homework 3: the boundary of each guarantee

Question. Which security property is actually implied by the result we proved, and which stronger claims would be unjustified?

HW3 · Problem 1(a)

An interactive proof has soundness error greater than \(1/3\): some prover convinces the verifier of a false statement with probability greater than \(1/3\). Can it have knowledge error less than \(1/3\)?

Reveal proof

No. Knowledge error \(\kappa\) is the threshold above which a proof-of-knowledge guarantee demands an efficient extractor recovering a valid witness from a successful prover. If \(\kappa<1/3\), then the false-statement prover with acceptance greater than \(1/3\) must have an extractable witness. But the statement is false, so no witness exists. This contradicts the extraction guarantee.

Therefore a protocol with the specified false-statement acceptance probability cannot have knowledge error below \(1/3\). The contradiction uses the meaning of a false statement, rather than merely recalling that knowledge implies soundness. \(\square\)

HW3 · Problem 1(b)

Can we construct non-interactive zero-knowledge proofs for NP in the standard model without a random oracle? Explain why the setup model changes the answer.

Reveal careful conditional answer

Distinguish the plain model, with no shared trusted reference information, from the common reference string (CRS) model. The standard model means we do not assume a random oracle; it need not exclude a CRS.

In the plain model, suppose a NIZK existed for every NP language. Given a statement \(x\), run its efficient zero-knowledge simulator without a witness to produce a purported proof, then run the verifier. On true \(x\), completeness and simulation ensure acceptance with high probability. On false \(x\), soundness limits acceptance of any efficient algorithm's proof, including the simulator's. After standard amplification, this yields a randomized polynomial-time (BPP) decision algorithm for the language.

Thus plain-model NIZKs for all NP would imply \(\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{BPP}\), which is not expected but is unproved to be false. In the CRS model, suitable standard-model NIZK constructions for NP are possible under cryptographic assumptions.

“Without a random oracle” by itself is insufficient to declare NIZK impossible; the exact absence or presence of a setup string is the decisive distinction. \(\square\)

HW3 · Problem 1(d)

True or false: applying Fiat–Shamir to a Sigma protocol always gives statistical zero-knowledge.

Reveal counterargument

False. Honest-verifier zero knowledge for a Sigma protocol may only be computational: no efficient distinguisher detects the witness, but an unbounded distinguisher might. Statistical zero-knowledge is a strictly stronger requirement that also limits unbounded distinguishers.

Fiat–Shamir merely replaces the challenge with a hash-derived one. It supplies no argument that the existing transcript distribution becomes statistically close to a witness-free distribution. Nor does HVZK alone settle malicious-verifier or non-interactive simulation security.

A change in challenge generation cannot, by itself, justify strengthening a computational privacy guarantee into a statistical one. \(\square\)

HW3 1(c), the \(\mathbb Z_6\) root-count counterexample, was solved in the finite-field lesson at exactly the point where its missing assumption becomes relevant.

Homework 3: amplify before hashing

Question. Why does changing the source of a one-bit challenge not fix constant soundness error, and how does parallel repetition fix the exact problem?

HW3 · Problem 3(a)

A Sigma protocol has a cheating strategy on a false statement that succeeds with probability \(1/2\) against a uniform one-bit challenge. Show why Fiat–Shamir applied directly does not obtain negligible soundness error.

Complete argument

Let the cheating prover construct its first message \(u\) using the given strategy and fix it. It now queries the random oracle at the public statement and \(u\) to obtain \(c=H(x,u)\). This challenge is uniformly random, exactly as in the original interactive soundness experiment, because the first message was fixed before the query.

The cheating prover calculates the strategy's response to that \(c\) and publishes a one-message proof. By the problem's assumption, the original cheating strategy succeeds with probability \(1/2\); the hash-derived experiment has the same challenge distribution, so it still succeeds with probability \(1/2\).

The false statement is accepted with constant probability, which is not negligible. The problem assumes an actual successful cheating strategy; an upper bound of \(1/2\) alone would not prove such an attack exists. \(\square\)

HW3 · Problem 3(b)

Build a three-message protocol with negligible soundness error from the one-bit-challenge Sigma protocol, and then make it non-interactive.

Complete protocol and counting proof

Protocol. For security parameter \(\lambda\), the prover sends \(\lambda\) first messages together. The verifier sends \(\lambda\) independent uniform challenge bits together. The prover sends the \(\lambda\) responses together. Accept only if every coordinate verifies. This is still a three-message protocol: the messages are batches.

Why the soundness error is \(2^{-\lambda}\). Fix any batch of first messages on a false statement. For a particular coordinate \(i\), if the prover could provide valid answers for both challenges 0 and 1 to the same \(u_i\), special soundness would extract a witness for that false statement—a contradiction. Thus each coordinate has at most one answerable challenge. The whole batch therefore has at most one answerable vector among the \(2^\lambda\) equally likely challenge vectors. With a uniformly independent challenge vector, the acceptance probability is at most \(2^{-\lambda}\), whether or not the dishonest prover correlated its initial messages.

Non-interactive version. Hash the public statement and the complete vector of first messages to derive the entire \(\lambda\)-bit challenge vector; then publish the first messages and responses. The verifier recomputes the vector and verifies every coordinate. In the random-oracle model, polynomially many commitment attempts cannot overcome an exponentially small per-attempt challenge-matching probability.

The bound succeeds because the fixed first-message vector can answer at most one of \(2^\lambda\) challenge vectors. Fiat–Shamir then removes the interaction without making that challenge vector smaller. \(\square\)

Homework 3: the non-interactive PCP argument

Question. The PCP/Merkle construction from Part I has three messages. Which message can be removed, and why does this preserve the reasoning of the original argument?

HW3 · Problem 4(b) · Original task

Convert the succinct interactive argument in Problem 4(a) into a non-interactive argument in the random-oracle model.

Complete construction and justification

The prover constructs the complete PCP proof string \(\pi_{\rm PCP}\) and its Merkle root \(c\). It derives the verifier randomness for all \(\lambda\) independent PCP tests as

\[\rho=H_{\rm challenge}(\operatorname{encode}(x,c,\mathrm{params})).\]

Here \(x\) is the public statement and \(\mathrm{params}\) contains the protocol settings; a domain-separated challenge oracle avoids confusing the Merkle hashing role with Fiat–Shamir. The deterministic PCP verifier's query algorithm applied to \(\rho\) produces the positions that would have been sent in the second message.

The prover now supplies a single proof string containing \(c\), all requested bit values and their Merkle authentication paths. A verifier recomputes \(\rho\) and the positions from \(x,c\), checks every authentication path against \(c\), and runs all \(\lambda\) PCP predicates. It accepts only if every check passes.

Completeness. Honest PCP bits satisfy all tests; honest Merkle paths authenticate. Both sides use the same deterministic hash-derived challenge, so an honest proof is accepted.

Soundness. The prover must choose \(c\) before discovering the random-oracle response attached to that commitment. Its authenticated openings are tied to one committed proof except with negligible probability of breaking binding. The amplified PCP test then has per-attempt error at most \(2^{-\lambda}\). A polynomial number \(Q\) of attempted commitments gives at most \(Q\,2^{-\lambda}+\operatorname{negl}(\lambda)\), using the random-oracle challenge distribution and a union bound.

Size. The proof contains one \(\lambda\)-bit root and \(O(\lambda)\) Merkle paths of size \(O(\lambda\log M)\), giving \(O(\lambda^2\log M)=O(\lambda^2\log n)\) communication, as in 4(a). There is no verifier-to-prover challenge message anymore.

The compiled system is a succinct, non-interactive argument: the commitment fixes the proof before the random test is determined, and Fiat–Shamir makes that test reproducible by every verifier. This construction has not established zero-knowledge or knowledge extraction. \(\square\)

The transition to PLONK. A PCP stores individual bits and authenticates their positions using Merkle paths. The lecture's modern construction uses polynomial IOPs instead: many circuit constraints become polynomial identities, authenticated by polynomial commitments. The same two-stage principle—information-theoretic verification first, cryptographic oracle replacement second—still applies.
