Lecture §3 · The computation to certify

From a long computation to equations we can check

After the PCP construction, the lecture changes the representation of the evidence. To see why, suppose a prover claims that the hundredth Fibonacci number is \(354\,224\,848\,179\,261\,915\,075\). Recomputing the hundred steps would establish the result, but we want the verifier to avoid performing every step.

The Fibonacci sequence begins \(0,1,1,2,3,5,\ldots\). Each value after the first two is the sum of its two predecessors. Let \(y_i\) denote the prover's proposed value at position \(i\), for \(i=0,\ldots,100\). To establish the claim we need exactly three kinds of facts:

\[ y_0=0,\quad y_1=1;\qquad y_{100}=354\,224\,848\,179\,261\,915\,075;\qquad y_{i+2}=y_{i+1}+y_i\ (0\le i\le98). \]

The first two conditions fix the endpoints. The last condition forces every intermediate value: from 0 and 1 it forces \(y_2=1\), then \(y_3=2\), and continues uniquely through \(y_{100}\). Without the last condition, the prover could simply write down the target and arbitrary intermediate values.

Source correction. The lecture states both initial values as 1 but quotes the conventional value of \(F_{100}\), which uses \(F_0=0,F_1=1\). We consistently use the latter convention. The proof method does not depend on which convention is chosen, provided the target matches it.

Checking 99 separate recurrence equations would still require work proportional to the computation. The polynomial approach will express the whole proposed sequence as one algebraic object and then reduce these many conditions to a test at a random point.

Before constructing that polynomial, we need arithmetic in which interpolation is possible and the verifier can choose a genuinely random evaluation point. That is why the lecture works over a finite field.

Lecture §3 · Field arithmetic as a prerequisite

Why finite-field arithmetic is needed

We want a polynomial whose value at each integer position matches one proposed Fibonacci value. A polynomial is an expression \(f(X)=a_0+a_1X+\cdots+a_dX^d\): each \(a_j\) is a coefficient and its degree \(d\) is the largest exponent with nonzero coefficient. Evaluating \(f(r)\) means replacing \(X\) with \(r\) and calculating.

Constructing a polynomial through chosen points requires division, while testing it probabilistically requires a finite collection of equally likely challenges. A finite field supplies both. The example we use is \(\mathbb F_p\), the set \(\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}\) with addition and multiplication modulo a prime \(p\). In this field every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse, so division by it is possible.

For example, in \(\mathbb F_{17}\), \(15+5=3\), because 20 leaves remainder 3 when divided by 17. Also, \(2^{-1}=9\), because \(2\cdot9=18\equiv1\pmod{17}\). Thus the expression \(1/2\) means the field element 9 in this example; it does not mean the real number 0.5.

To represent the 101 Fibonacci positions without two different positions collapsing into the same field element, choose \(p>100\). To interpret the final result as the displayed integer rather than merely its residue modulo \(p\), choose \(p\) larger than the claimed Fibonacci values. Later, a much larger field will also make accidental agreement of two different polynomials unlikely.

All subsequent polynomial equations use field arithmetic, even when we write familiar integer symbols. We may use the small field \(\mathbb F_{17}\) in a figure to make the arithmetic visible, but that figure is not a proof of the 100th integer value.

Now we have the operations needed to fit a polynomial through several specified points. We start with three Fibonacci values, where the entire construction can be written explicitly.

Lecture §3 · Polynomial interpolation

Construct the first three encoded values

The prover wants a polynomial \(f(X)\) satisfying \(f(0)=0\), \(f(1)=1\), and \(f(2)=1\), the first three Fibonacci values. The simplest attempt is the line \(f(X)=aX+b\). The condition at 0 forces \(b=0\); the condition at 1 then forces \(a=1\). But the line \(f(X)=X\) gives \(f(2)=2\), not 1.

We need to alter the value at 2 without disturbing the values at 0 and 1. The product \(X(X-1)\) is zero at both earlier positions, so adding any multiple of that product leaves them unchanged. Write

\[f(X)=X+cX(X-1).\]

At \(X=2\), this becomes \(2+2c=1\), so \(c=-1/2\). Because our field has characteristic different from 2, division by 2 is allowed. The resulting polynomial is

\[\boxed{f(X)=X-\tfrac12X(X-1).}\]

Direct substitution verifies \(f(0)=0\), \(f(1)=1\), and \(f(2)=1\). The polynomial therefore records the proposed values. It does not prove they are correct: the same procedure fits any three values, even a dishonest proposal.

Interpolation fits honest and dishonest values

Start with 0, 1, 1. Change a claimed value and see that a different polynomial still fits all three points. This is why interpolation alone is not a proof.

The construction succeeded because the correction term vanished at every position we had already fitted. That gives a procedure for extending the polynomial from three values to all 101, which we derive next.

Lecture §3 · Interpolation through arbitrary points

One polynomial can record the entire proposed sequence

Suppose we have already built a polynomial \(f_j(X)\) matching the prover's proposed values \(y_0,\ldots,y_j\) at positions \(0,\ldots,j\). We now want its value at the next position \(j+1\) to be \(y_{j+1}\), while keeping all earlier values fixed.

Multiply together one factor for each earlier point:

\[P_j(X)=\prod_{i=0}^{j}(X-i).\]

At every earlier position \(i\), the factor \(X-i\) becomes zero, so \(P_j(i)=0\). At position \(j+1\), all factors are nonzero and the field permits division by their product. Thus choose the correction coefficient

\[ c_j=\frac{y_{j+1}-f_j(j+1)}{P_j(j+1)},\qquad f_{j+1}(X)=f_j(X)+c_jP_j(X). \]

The numerator is precisely the value we need to add at the new position. Multiplying \(c_j\) by \(P_j(j+1)\) produces that correction. At earlier positions the added term is zero. This proves that \(f_{j+1}\) matches all \(j+2\) values. Repeating gives a degree-at-most-100 polynomial \(f_{\rm fib}\) through the proposed 101 values. This is polynomial interpolation.

But the polynomial can equally well encode a false sequence. To test the recurrence, we define the discrepancy polynomial

\[ D(X)=f_{\rm fib}(X+2)-f_{\rm fib}(X+1)-f_{\rm fib}(X). \]

At each step position \(i=0,\ldots,98\), the required recurrence is equivalent to \(D(i)=0\). We have represented 99 checks as 99 required zeros of one polynomial, but we still need to test them without evaluating at 99 points.

A random evaluation can distinguish different polynomials only if they cannot coincide at too many points. The next theorem establishes exactly how many roots a nonzero polynomial can have.

Lecture §3 · Theorem 1 · Polynomial roots

How many points can hide a false polynomial equation?

The Fibonacci discrepancy \(D(X)\) measures whether each recurrence step is correct. We would like to check polynomial equations without examining every coefficient. Suppose two fixed polynomials \(f\) and \(g\) are different. How many evaluation points could make them appear equal?

If the verifier compares \(f(r)\) and \(g(r)\) at a chosen point \(r\), a false equality passes exactly when \(h(r)=0\), where \(h(X)=f(X)-g(X)\) is their difference polynomial. A point where a polynomial equals zero is called a root. We therefore need to count the roots of a nonzero polynomial.

Theorem 1: a nonzero degree-\(d\) polynomial has at most \(d\) roots

Suppose \(h(a)=0\). For any positive integer \(j\), the difference \(X^j-a^j\) contains the factor \(X-a\): multiplying \((X-a)(X^{j-1}+aX^{j-2}+\cdots+a^{j-1})\) cancels the intermediate terms and leaves \(X^j-a^j\). Apply this to every term of \(h(X)-h(a)\). Since \(h(a)=0\), we obtain \(h(X)=(X-a)q(X)\), where \(q\) has degree one less than \(h\).

If \(b\ne a\) is another root, substitute it into that equation:

\[0=h(b)=(b-a)q(b).\]

The factor \(b-a\) is nonzero. In a field, a product can be zero only if one factor is zero, so \(q(b)=0\). In other words, after removing the factor for root \(a\), the other roots remain roots of the quotient.

Each new distinct root therefore supplies another linear factor and reduces the degree by one. A nonzero degree-\(d\) polynomial cannot have more than \(d\) different linear factors.

Thus it has at most \(d\) distinct roots. The proof depends on being able to cancel the nonzero factor \(b-a\), which explains the field assumption. \(\square\)

The same theorem settles a question from interpolation: if two polynomials of degree at most \(N-1\) agree at \(N\) distinct points, their difference has at least \(N\) roots. The root bound forces that difference to be zero. Therefore the interpolating polynomial is unique.

Does the polynomial root bound hold modulo 6?

Consider \(f(X)=2X^2+4X\) with arithmetic modulo 6. It has degree 2. Could it have more than two roots (inputs where it evaluates to zero)? Test all six possible values of \(X\).

Find every root modulo 6

Change \(r\) and inspect the remainder of \(2r^2+4r\).

Work out the counterexample

The values 0, 1, 3 and 4 all make the polynomial zero modulo 6. So a degree-two polynomial has four different roots! Why doesn't that contradict the rule we want to use?

Because arithmetic modulo 6 is not a field. In particular \(2\cdot3\equiv0\pmod6\) although neither 2 nor 3 is zero. We cannot cancel nonzero factors here. The ordinary proof that a degree-\(d\) polynomial has at most \(d\) roots depends on being able to cancel such factors.

The root-count claim is false over \(\mathbb Z_6\), and this shows exactly which algebraic assumption fails. The later soundness proofs will work over fields instead. \(\square\)

We have proved the property needed for random verification: a nonzero degree-\(d\) polynomial has at most \(d\) roots. The next lesson applies that result to the verifier's actual equality test and derives its error probability.

Lecture §3 · EqualityTest and Lemma 1

How can one random evaluation test equality?

The previous lesson proved that a nonzero polynomial of degree at most \(d\) has at most \(d\) roots. We can now use that result to decide whether two polynomials fixed by the prover are identical, without checking every coefficient.

The verifier's test

Let \(f\) and \(g\) both have degree at most \(d\), and let the verifier choose \(r\) uniformly at random from a finite field \(\mathbb F\). It accepts if \(f(r)=g(r)\).

Completeness: if \(f=g\), the equality holds at every point, so the verifier always accepts.

Soundness: if \(f\ne g\), then \(h=f-g\) is a nonzero polynomial of degree at most \(d\). The verifier accepts incorrectly only if it chooses one of the at most \(d\) roots of \(h\). There are \(|\mathbb F|\) equally likely field values, so

\[\boxed{\Pr[f(r)=g(r)\mid f\ne g]\le\frac{d}{|\mathbb F|}.}\]

That is Lemma 1 in the lecture: perfect completeness and soundness error at most \(d/|\mathbb F|\).

Find exactly which checks miss a false claim

Over \(\mathbb F_{17}\), compare \(f(X)=X^2+1\) with \(g(X)=f(X)+(X-1)(X-4)\). They're different, but agree at two points.

There is one further condition that makes the probability argument work. The polynomials must be fixed before the verifier chooses \(r\). Otherwise a cheater who learns \(r\) could invent \(g(X)=f(X)+(X-r)\), which is a different polynomial but guaranteed to agree with \(f\) at that already-known point. Later we'll use commitments to enforce the correct order.

EqualityTest provides the missing testing method: if two low-degree polynomials are fixed before the challenge, one random evaluation can reveal a false identity with high probability. But Fibonacci's recurrence is required only at the 99 step positions, not at every field element. We still need to turn those 99 local conditions into a genuine polynomial identity. The lecture next generalizes the root argument to several variables. We will then return to the Fibonacci obstacle.

Lecture §3 · Lemma 2, Schwartz–Zippel

What changes when the equation has more than one variable?

EqualityTest gives the bound we need when an identity depends on one variable. Before we apply it to Fibonacci, the lecture establishes the more general Schwartz–Zippel lemma for equations involving several variables. This is a generalization, not a new requirement of Fibonacci: our recurrence polynomial still has only one variable. We prove the general result because the same reasoning is useful in other polynomial verification problems.

Take \(h(X,Y)=XY\) over \(\mathbb F_7\). The polynomial is zero if \(X=0\) or \(Y=0\). If we choose each coordinate independently from the seven field elements, there are \(7\times7=49\) equally likely pairs. Seven have \(X=0\), seven have \(Y=0\), and \((0,0)\) was counted twice. So exactly 13 pairs make this nonzero polynomial zero.

For multivariable polynomials, we need a measure of degree. In each term, add the exponents of its variables, then take the largest result. For instance \(3X^2Y^3Z+2XY^2\) has total degree \(2+3+1=6\).

Now we can state Lemma 2, Schwartz–Zippel. Let \(h(X_1,\ldots,X_m)\) be a nonzero polynomial in \(m\) variables, of total degree at most \(d\). Choose each \(r_i\) independently and uniformly from a finite subset \(\Omega\) of the field. Then

\[\boxed{\Pr[h(r_1,\ldots,r_m)=0]\le\frac{d}{|\Omega|}.}\]

It is the same sort of bound as before. The proof explains why the number of variables does not suddenly make the chance of a false acceptance large.

Proof: remove one variable at a time

For one variable, this is the root-count result we've already proved. Suppose the result holds for \(m-1\) variables. Collect the powers of the last variable \(X_m\):

\[h(X_1,\ldots,X_m)=\sum_{j=0}^{t}g_j(X_1,\ldots,X_{m-1})X_m^j,\qquad g_t\ne0.\]

The last coefficient \(g_t\) is nonzero, because \(t\) is the highest power that actually occurs. Since \(h\) has total degree at most \(d\), \(g_t\) has degree at most \(d-t\).

First choose random values for the first \(m-1\) variables. There is a chance that \(g_t\) evaluates to zero; by our induction assumption, that chance is at most \((d-t)/|\Omega|\).

Otherwise \(g_t\) remains nonzero. Once those first values are fixed, the original \(h\) becomes a nonzero polynomial of degree \(t\) in the single variable \(X_m\). The ordinary root bound says the last random choice \(r_m\) can make it zero with conditional probability at most \(t/|\Omega|\).

Every way the full expression can become zero falls into these cases. Adding their probability bounds gives

\[\Pr[h(\mathbf r)=0]\le\frac{d-t}{|\Omega|}+\frac{t}{|\Omega|}=\frac{d}{|\Omega|}.\]

We have proved the lemma by induction from the one-variable root theorem. As before, a soundness proof must first establish that a false claim produces a nonzero polynomial. \(\square\)

For the example \(XY\), the total degree is 2, so the lemma gives the bound \(2/7=14/49\). We counted 13 zeros. The bound is correct, and it applies to every nonzero polynomial with the stated total degree, not just this example.

II · Polynomial reasoning · 2025 midterm, Problem 3(c.1)

Exam problem: can a vector hide more than one vote?

This problem is important because the arithmetic is simple but the logic must be exact. You have to prove that every invalid vector gives a nonzero polynomial—not just the invalid examples you can think of.

Previous midterm · Problem 3(c.1) · Four marks

We work in \(\mathbb Z_p\), for an odd prime \(p\). A vector \(\mathbf x=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)\) is allowed to be either all zero, or a standard basis vector \(\mathbf e_i\), which has exactly one entry equal to 1 and all others zero.

The verifier chooses \(\mathbf r=(r_1,\ldots,r_N)\) uniformly at random and makes \(\mathbf r_{\mathrm{sq}}=(r_1^2,\ldots,r_N^2)\). It accepts if

\[ \langle\mathbf x,\mathbf r\rangle^2= \langle\mathbf x,\mathbf r_{\mathrm{sq}}\rangle. \]

The angle brackets mean \(\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf r\rangle=\sum_i x_i r_i\): multiply matching entries and add. Prove perfect completeness and soundness error at most \(2/p\).

Show every step of the proof

1. An allowed vector always passes

If \(\mathbf x=\mathbf0\), both sums are zero. If \(\mathbf x=\mathbf e_j\), the left inner product picks out \(r_j\), and squaring it gives \(r_j^2\). The right inner product also picks out \(r_j^2\). So every allowed vector passes for every choice of \(\mathbf r\). That's perfect completeness.

2. What would make an invalid vector pass?

Put both sides of the acceptance equation on one side:

\[ h(\mathbf r)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^N x_i r_i\right)^2 -\sum_{i=1}^N x_i r_i^2. \]

For the fixed vector \(\mathbf x\), this is a polynomial in the random numbers \(r_i\). The verifier accepts exactly when \(h(\mathbf r)=0\). It has total degree at most 2. We still need to show it is nonzero for every invalid vector.

3. Every invalid vector falls into one of two cases

Case A: exactly one entry is nonzero. Say \(x_j=a\). Since the vector is invalid, \(a\ne1\); since it's the nonzero entry, \(a\ne0\). Then

\[ h(\mathbf r)=(ar_j)^2-ar_j^2 =a(a-1)r_j^2. \]

Neither \(a\) nor \(a-1\) is zero, so their product is nonzero in a field. Thus \(h\) is not the zero polynomial.

Case B: two or more entries are nonzero. Choose two, \(x_i\ne0\) and \(x_j\ne0\), with \(i\ne j\). Squaring the first sum creates the term \(2x_ix_jr_ir_j\). The second sum contains only squared terms \(r_k^2\), so it cannot cancel a term containing \(r_ir_j\) with two distinct indices. Since \(p\) is odd, \(2x_ix_j\ne0\). Again \(h\) is nonzero.

These cases exhaust the invalid possibilities. The all-zero vector is valid; any other invalid vector has either one nonzero entry that is not 1, or more than one nonzero entry.

4. Apply Schwartz–Zippel

For every invalid vector we've proved that \(h\) is nonzero and has total degree at most 2. Each random coordinate comes from the \(p\)-element field. So

\[ \boxed{\Pr[\text{invalid vector passes}]= \Pr[h(\mathbf r)=0]\le\frac2p.} \]

That's the soundness proof. We established the precise polynomial whose zero means acceptance, showed it can't be identically zero for an invalid vote, and then used its degree to bound false acceptance. \(\square\)

The assumption that \(p\) is odd matters. If \(p=2\), the coefficient 2 in Case B becomes zero, and that argument no longer works.

Lecture §3 · Vanishing polynomials and Theorem 2

How can 99 recurrence equations become one identity?

The verifier needs to know that the Fibonacci discrepancy \(D(i)\) is zero for every \(i=0,\ldots,98\). EqualityTest checks an identity that holds everywhere, but our recurrence is only required at these particular positions. We must first turn those 99 conditions into one identity that is true everywhere exactly when all the conditions hold.

Recall the discrepancy polynomial:

\[ D(X)=f_{\rm fib}(X+2)-f_{\rm fib}(X+1)-f_{\rm fib}(X). \]

For each required step \(i\), the recurrence is correct if and only if \(D(i)=0\). Let \(\Omega=\{0,1,\ldots,98\}\) be the set of positions where this must happen. We want a polynomial that is zero at precisely these designated points.

Build a polynomial with the required roots

For one required point \(a\), the factor \(X-a\) is zero at \(X=a\). To make a polynomial zero at every point of \(\Omega\), multiply one factor for each point:

\[ \boxed{Z_\Omega(X)=\prod_{a\in\Omega}(X-a).} \]

This is the vanishing polynomial of \(\Omega\). For the Fibonacci steps, it is \(X(X-1)\cdots(X-98)\). The symbol \(\prod\) means to multiply all the listed factors. The polynomial has degree \(|\Omega|=99\), because it is a product of 99 linear factors.

Why is this useful? If \(D(X)\) contains every factor of \(Z_\Omega(X)\), then it must be zero at every required position. The converse is also true, and proving it is what allows us to replace 99 equations by one.

Theorem 2: zeros on \(\Omega\) are equivalent to divisibility by \(Z_\Omega\)

Forward direction. Suppose \(D(X)=Z_\Omega(X)q(X)\) for some polynomial \(q\). At any \(a\in\Omega\), one factor of \(Z_\Omega(a)\) is \(a-a=0\). Therefore \(D(a)=Z_\Omega(a)q(a)=0\). Thus every required recurrence step holds.

Reverse direction. Suppose \(D(a)=0\) for every \(a\in\Omega\). By the factor theorem, each such root supplies a factor \(X-a\). Because the points are distinct, after dividing out the factor for one root, every other root remains a root of the quotient: this follows from the same cancellation argument used in Theorem 1. Repeating for all points gives

\[ D(X)=\left(\prod_{a\in\Omega}(X-a)\right)q(X) =Z_\Omega(X)q(X). \]

If \(D\) is identically zero, choose \(q=0\). Both directions are proved. Hence \(D\) is zero on \(\Omega\) if and only if it is divisible by \(Z_\Omega\). \(\square\)

Check the equivalence on a small example

Take \(\Omega=\{1,2\}\) and \(D(X)=X^3-7X+6\). Factoring gives \(D(X)=(X-1)(X-2)(X+3)\). Therefore \(Z_\Omega(X)=(X-1)(X-2)\) and the quotient is \(q(X)=X+3\). The two required zeros are present precisely because the vanishing polynomial divides \(D\).

This example is not the general proof; it simply shows how the theorem can be used to construct a quotient.

We have now achieved the crucial algebraic step. Instead of asking the verifier to check 99 separate values \(D(i)=0\), the prover can claim that there exists a polynomial \(q\) satisfying the global identity \(D(X)=Z_\Omega(X)q(X)\). The verifier can test that identity at one random point. We call the resulting protocol ZeroTest.

Lecture §3 · ZeroTest and Lemma 3

How can the verifier check the quotient identity?

The vanishing-polynomial theorem tells us that all required zeros are present exactly when \(f(X)=Z_\Omega(X)q(X)\) for some polynomial \(q\). The prover can supply a proposed quotient. Now we must check that the identity is genuine without reading every coefficient or testing every required point.

The protocol in the ideal polynomial oracle

The lecture states the test for a general polynomial \(f\) of degree at most \(d\) and a set \(\Omega\) of \(k\le d\) field points. The prover fixes \(f\) and a claimed quotient \(q\) in the oracle, with degree at most \(d\) for the quotient. The verifier chooses a uniformly random field point \(r\), requests \(f(r)\) and \(q(r)\), computes the known vanishing polynomial \(Z_\Omega(r)\), and accepts if

\[\boxed{f(r)=q(r)Z_\Omega(r).}\]

Why choose \(r\) only after the polynomials are fixed? If the prover learned \(r\) first, it could invent a quotient whose value at that point makes the equation pass. The random-root bound applies only to one fixed difference polynomial.

Try an honest and a dishonest quotient identity

Over \(\mathbb F_{17}\), the required zeros are at 1 and 2. Compare the honest polynomial \(f=Z_\Omega q\) with a dishonest polynomial that differs by \(X-3\). At \(r=3\), the false identity happens to pass.

Prove completeness and soundness

Lemma 3: perfect completeness and error at most \(2d/|\mathbb F|\)

Completeness. If \(f\) is zero at every point of \(\Omega\), Theorem 2 guarantees a polynomial \(q\) with \(f=Z_\Omega q\). The honest prover supplies that quotient. The identity then holds for every possible \(r\), so the verifier always accepts.

Soundness. Suppose \(f\) is not zero at every point of \(\Omega\). By Theorem 2, no polynomial quotient can make the identity true everywhere. For any fixed cheating quotient \(q\), define

\[h(X)=f(X)-q(X)Z_\Omega(X).\]

This polynomial is nonzero, because otherwise \(f=Z_\Omega q\), contradicting the false claim. We must also bound its degree. The polynomial \(f\) has degree at most \(d\), the quotient \(q\) has degree at most \(d\), and \(Z_\Omega\) has degree \(k\le d\). Therefore \(qZ_\Omega\) has degree at most \(d+k\le2d\), and so does \(h\).

The verifier accepts the false claim only when \(h(r)=0\). The root theorem says a nonzero polynomial of degree at most \(2d\) has at most \(2d\) roots. With \(r\) uniform in \(\mathbb F\),

\[\boxed{\Pr[\text{false ZeroTest acceptance}]\le\frac{2d}{|\mathbb F|}.}\]

This proves the lecture's Lemma 3. The proof succeeds because a false vanishing claim produces a fixed, nonzero, low-degree difference polynomial. \(\square\)

The bound may be uninformative if the field is too small. A cryptographic implementation uses a sufficiently large field and enforces the polynomial degree bounds; our small numerical example is only a demonstration.

Lecture §3 · The complete Fibonacci Poly-IOP

Do the polynomial checks really prove the claimed number?

We can now combine the algebraic tools into a verification protocol. The prover supplies a polynomial representing its proposed Fibonacci sequence and a quotient for the recurrence discrepancy. The verifier checks the endpoints and one random identity. We must show that passing these checks forces the original public claim, rather than merely producing plausible equations.

What the prover prepares

Let \(f_{\rm fib}\) be the degree-at-most-100 polynomial representing the claimed values at positions \(0,\ldots,100\). Define the recurrence discrepancy and the vanishing polynomial for the 99 required steps:

\[ \begin{aligned} D(X)&=f_{\rm fib}(X+2)-f_{\rm fib}(X+1)-f_{\rm fib}(X),\\ \Omega&=\{0,1,\ldots,98\},\\ Z_\Omega(X)&=\prod_{i=0}^{98}(X-i). \end{aligned} \]

If the proposed sequence is correct, \(D\) vanishes at every point of \(\Omega\), so Theorem 2 gives a quotient polynomial \(q\) satisfying \(D=Z_\Omega q\). The prover fixes \(f_{\rm fib}\) and \(q\) in the ideal polynomial oracle before the verifier chooses a random challenge.

What the verifier checks

First, it requests the evaluations at positions 0, 1 and 100. These must equal the public starting values and claimed final result:

\[ f_{\rm fib}(0)=0,\quad f_{\rm fib}(1)=1,\quad f_{\rm fib}(100)=354\,224\,848\,179\,261\,915\,075. \]

Second, it chooses a uniform random field element \(r\), requests \(f_{\rm fib}(r)\), \(f_{\rm fib}(r+1)\), \(f_{\rm fib}(r+2)\), and \(q(r)\), and checks the ZeroTest identity

\[ \boxed{ f_{\rm fib}(r+2)-f_{\rm fib}(r+1)-f_{\rm fib}(r) =q(r)\prod_{i=0}^{98}(r-i). } \]

The left side is exactly \(D(r)\); the right side is exactly \(q(r)Z_\Omega(r)\). We have not invented a new test here: we are applying the ZeroTest whose soundness was proved in the preceding lesson.

Why the recurrence fixes every subsequent value

Starting from 0 and 1, move through a short Fibonacci sequence. Each new value is forced by the preceding two; there is no freedom to change one value while keeping all recurrence equations true.

Lecture §3 · Completeness, soundness and remaining costs

Why does the verifier's check establish the claimed value?

The verifier checks the starting values, the claimed final value, and one random recurrence identity. We must show why these checks force the intended sequence, then identify which parts of the ideal protocol still require cryptography or faster arithmetic.

Why the checks establish the public statement

Suppose the endpoint checks hold and the recurrence identity holds as a polynomial identity. By Theorem 2, the discrepancy is zero at every required step. Starting with \(f_{\rm fib}(0)=0\) and \(f_{\rm fib}(1)=1\), the recurrence forces \(f_{\rm fib}(2)=1\). Once positions 0 through \(i+1\) are fixed, the recurrence at position \(i\) forces the value at \(i+2\). By induction, the polynomial's values at all positions through 100 equal the Fibonacci sequence.

The final endpoint check then establishes that the actual hundredth value equals the public target. Conversely, if the public target is false, the verifier cannot have both correct endpoints and all correct recurrence equations. A false endpoint is rejected directly; otherwise the recurrence is false somewhere and the ZeroTest soundness bound applies.

Thus the ideal protocol has perfect completeness and the same bounded false-acceptance probability as the recurrence ZeroTest, assuming the polynomial oracle enforces the required degree and consistency restrictions. \(\square\)

Why isn't this a finished SNARG?

Two problems remain. First, the oracle is imaginary: a real prover could invent evaluations after seeing \(r\). We need a polynomial commitment to fix the polynomial and authenticate its answers.

Second, evaluating \(Z_\Omega(r)\) directly requires multiplying 99 factors. For a computation with millions of steps, that would take work proportional to the computation size. The lecture later uses structured evaluation points or circuit-specific preprocessing to make this calculation efficient.

We have proved the mathematical checking method. The next section solves the first remaining problem: replacing the ideal polynomial oracle with a cryptographic commitment that supports short, authenticated evaluation proofs. The efficient vanishing-polynomial calculation will return when we build PLONK.
