Lecture §3 · The Magic of Polynomials

Can we check the hundredth Fibonacci number without computing it?

The PCP construction showed that a verifier can sometimes check a long proof by asking only a few questions. The lecture now develops a different method for arithmetic computations. We will represent the computation's intermediate values by a polynomial, then try to check their relationships without reading every value.

The prover claims that the hundredth Fibonacci number is \(354\,224\,848\,179\,261\,915\,075\). Every Fibonacci number is the sum of the preceding two. We could calculate all the numbers ourselves, but that defeats our purpose.

What must a convincing proof establish?

Imagine a function \(f_{\rm fib}\) whose value at position \(i\) is the number the prover claims for that step. We need three conditions: the first two values are correct, the value at position 100 is the public target, and every intervening value follows the recurrence.

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{\rm fib}(0)&=0,\qquad f_{\rm fib}(1)=1,\\ f_{\rm fib}(100)&=354\,224\,848\,179\,261\,915\,075,\\ f_{\rm fib}(i+2)&=f_{\rm fib}(i+1)+f_{\rm fib}(i) \quad(i=0,\ldots,98). \end{aligned} \]

Why are these conditions sufficient? Starting with 0 and 1, the recurrence forces the value at 2, then at 3, and so on. No later value can be chosen independently. Thus, if all the equations hold, the value at position 100 must be the actual hundredth Fibonacci number.

Inconsistency in the official notes: the lecture writes \(f_{\rm fib}(0)=f_{\rm fib}(1)=1\), but its numerical target uses the convention \(F_0=0,F_1=1\). We use the latter so the stated numerical claim is consistent. The verification argument works under either convention, but the indexed final value changes.

We have not yet saved work. Checking the 99 recurrence equations directly is still expensive. The proposed shortcut is to encode the entire sequence in a polynomial and then test a small number of polynomial equations.

Why does the arithmetic use a finite field?

A polynomial is an expression such as \(f(X)=a_0+a_1X+\cdots+a_dX^d\). The coefficients are \(a_0,\ldots,a_d\); the degree is the highest exponent with a nonzero coefficient. Evaluating at \(r\) means substituting \(X=r\).

We will need to construct a polynomial through prescribed values, which requires dividing by nonzero numbers. Later, the verifier will choose a random evaluation point, so we need a finite set of equally likely possibilities. A finite field gives us both ordinary division by nonzero elements and a finite set from which to sample.

For a prime \(p\), the field \(\mathbb F_p\) consists of \(0,1,\ldots,p-1\), with arithmetic modulo \(p\). For example, \(15+5=3\) in \(\mathbb F_{17}\), because 20 has remainder 3. Also, \(5^{-1}=7\) in this field, because \(5\cdot7\equiv1\pmod{17}\). An inverse lets us divide without leaving the field.

For the real Fibonacci example, the positions 0 through 100 must be distinct field elements, so \(p>100\). To prove the displayed integer value rather than only its remainder modulo \(p\), we can choose a field large enough to avoid wraparound of the relevant sequence values. The small fields in our figures illustrate the algebra only.

The field gives us the arithmetic needed to construct and test a polynomial. Next we will actually construct one through the first Fibonacci values. That solves the representation problem, not yet the problem of proving the recurrence.

Lecture §3 · Interpolating the computation

How can one polynomial represent the Fibonacci sequence?

The prover has a proposed value at every Fibonacci position. We want one polynomial that returns those values when evaluated at the corresponding positions. We will construct it for three values first, then extend the same argument to all 101 values.

Fit the first three values

We need \(f_2(0)=0\), \(f_2(1)=1\), and \(f_2(2)=1\). Try a line \(f_2(X)=aX+b\). The first condition gives \(b=0\), and the second gives \(a=1\). But the resulting line \(X\) gives 2 at position 2, not 1.

We need to correct the value at 2 without changing the first two. The product \(X(X-1)\) equals zero at positions 0 and 1. Adding a multiple of it therefore preserves those two values:

\[f_2(X)=X+cX(X-1).\]

The new condition \(f_2(2)=1\) gives \(2+2c=1\), so \(c=-1/2\). Division by 2 is permitted in a field of characteristic different from 2. Therefore

\[\boxed{f_2(X)=X-\frac12X(X-1).}\]

Substitution gives \(f_2(0)=0\), \(f_2(1)=1\), and \(f_2(2)=1\). We have fitted the three values. We have not proved that they satisfy the Fibonacci recurrence.

Interpolation fits honest and dishonest values

Start with 0, 1, 1. Change a claimed value and see that a different polynomial still fits all three points. This is why interpolation alone is not a proof.

Extend the construction to all 101 values

Suppose a polynomial \(f_j\) already fits positions \(0,\ldots,j\). To fit the next position \(j+1\), add a multiple of the product that vanishes at every earlier position:

\[f_{j+1}(X)=f_j(X)+c_j\prod_{i=0}^{j}(X-i).\]

The product is zero at positions \(0,\ldots,j\), so those values do not change. At \(j+1\), the product is nonzero because the positions are distinct field elements. We can divide the required correction by that product to choose \(c_j\). Repeating this gives a polynomial of degree at most 100 through all 101 values. This construction is called interpolation.

We will prove later that the degree-at-most-100 polynomial is unique. More importantly, it need not be short: it can have 101 coefficients. The benefit is not compression. The benefit is that the verifier can now describe the recurrence as an equation involving evaluations of one polynomial.

What remains to be checked?

For each step \(i\), define the recurrence discrepancy as the amount by which the claimed next value differs from the sum of its two predecessors:

\[D(i)=f_{\rm fib}(i+2)-f_{\rm fib}(i+1)-f_{\rm fib}(i).\]

The recurrence holds precisely when \(D(i)=0\) for every \(i=0,\ldots,98\). A dishonest sequence can be interpolated, but it cannot make these equations true unless it follows the recurrence.

We still cannot afford to check 99 discrepancies separately. The next theorem tells us why a single random evaluation can test a polynomial identity with a small error probability. After proving that, we will turn the 99 required zeros into one identity.

Lecture §3 · EqualityTest and the polynomial root theorem

Can one random point test an entire polynomial identity?

We have a polynomial that represents the prover's Fibonacci sequence, but a false sequence can be interpolated just as easily as a correct one. We need a way to check equations about that polynomial without reading all its coefficients. Start with a simpler problem: how can we check whether two fixed polynomials are identical?

Let \(f(X)\) and \(g(X)\) be polynomials over a field \(\mathbb F\), both of degree at most \(d\). If we compared their values at \(d+1\) different points, the root theorem below would make equality certain. But \(d+1\) evaluations would be expensive for a large computation. We will try just one random point.

EqualityTest: what the verifier does

The prover fixes \(f\) and \(g\) in the ideal polynomial oracle. The verifier then chooses \(r\) uniformly from \(\mathbb F\), requests \(f(r)\) and \(g(r)\), and accepts when \(f(r)=g(r)\). The polynomials must be fixed before

If \(f=g\), every possible evaluation agrees, so an honest proof always passes. If \(f\ne g\), define the difference polynomial \(h(X)=f(X)-g(X)\). The verifier accepts a false equality precisely when \(h(r)=0\). Such a point is called a root of \(h\). We therefore need to know how many roots a nonzero polynomial can have.

Theorem 1: a nonzero degree-\(d\) polynomial has at most \(d\) roots

Suppose \(h(a)=0\). For every integer \(k\ge1\), the expression \(X^k-a^k\) has a factor \(X-a\). Applying this to the terms of \(h(X)-h(a)\), and using \(h(a)=0\), shows that \(h(X)=(X-a)q(X)\) for some polynomial \(q\) of degree \(d-1\).

If \(b\ne a\) is another root, then \(0=h(b)=(b-a)q(b)\). Because \(b-a\ne0\) and we are in a field, we can cancel it and obtain \(q(b)=0\). Thus every additional distinct root is a root of the lower-degree polynomial \(q\).

Repeat this argument: each distinct root contributes another factor of degree one. A nonzero degree-\(d\) polynomial cannot have more than \(d\) such factors. Therefore it has at most \(d\) roots. \(\square\)

Lemma 1: the precise soundness guarantee

For a false equality, \(h=f-g\) is nonzero and has degree at most \(d\). The theorem says at most \(d\) of the \(|\mathbb F|\) equally likely choices of \(r\) can make \(h(r)=0\). Therefore

\[ \boxed{\Pr[f(r)=g(r)\mid f\ne g]\le\frac{d}{|\mathbb F|}.} \]

This proves Lemma 1: perfect completeness and soundness error at most \(d/|\mathbb F|\). A sufficiently large field makes this error small. The root theorem also proves interpolation uniqueness: if two degree-at-most-\(d\) polynomials agreed at \(d+1\) distinct points, their nonzero difference would have too many roots, which is impossible.

Find the exceptional points

In \(\mathbb F_{17}\), compare \(f(X)=X^2+1\) with \(g(X)=f(X)+(X-1)(X-4)\). They are different polynomials, but their evaluations agree at exactly two points.

The figure exhibits two roots of one difference polynomial. It is an example, not a proof of the general bound; the theorem above supplies that proof.

Lecture §3 · EqualityTest, Theorem 1 and Lemma 1

If two polynomials agree once, are they equal?

We have a polynomial that represents the proposed Fibonacci sequence. The next task is to check its recurrence. At every required position, the next value must equal the sum of the preceding two. Before combining those checks, we need to know whether one random evaluation can detect a false polynomial identity.

\[D(X)=f_{\rm fib}(X+2)-f_{\rm fib}(X+1)-f_{\rm fib}(X).\]

We need \(D(i)=0\) at every required step. Eventually we'll express those many requirements as one polynomial identity. But first we need to know whether a verifier can test any fixed polynomial identity without inspecting every coefficient. That is the simpler problem EqualityTest solves.

One matching value doesn't prove equality with certainty. For example, \(f(X)=0\) and \(g(X)=X-3\) differ as polynomials but both give zero at \(X=3\). The useful question is not whether they could match by accident; it is how many choices of \(r\) would make that accident happen.

Call \(h(X)=f(X)-g(X)\) the difference. The verifier is fooled exactly when \(h(r)=0\). A number that makes a polynomial zero is called a root. So we need to know how many roots a nonzero polynomial can have.

Theorem 1: a nonzero degree-\(d\) polynomial has at most \(d\) roots

Suppose \(h(a)=0\). Every expression \(X^j-a^j\) is divisible by \(X-a\): for example \(X^2-a^2=(X-a)(X+a)\). By applying this fact to each term, \(h(X)-h(a)\) is divisible by \(X-a\). Since \(h(a)=0\), we can write \(h(X)=(X-a)q(X)\).

If \(b\ne a\) is another root, substitute it into that equation:

\[0=h(b)=(b-a)q(b).\]

The factor \(b-a\) is nonzero. In a field, a product can be zero only if one factor is zero, so \(q(b)=0\). In other words, after removing the factor for root \(a\), the other roots remain roots of the quotient.

Each new distinct root therefore supplies another linear factor and reduces the degree by one. A nonzero degree-\(d\) polynomial cannot have more than \(d\) different linear factors.

Thus it has at most \(d\) distinct roots. The proof depends on being able to cancel the nonzero factor \(b-a\), which explains the field assumption. \(\square\)

There is one further condition that makes the probability argument work. The polynomials must be fixed before the verifier chooses \(r\). Later we'll use commitments to enforce the correct order.

But Fibonacci's recurrence is required only at the 99 step positions, not at every field element. We still need to turn those 99 local conditions into a genuine polynomial identity. Lecture §3 · Schwartz–Zippel lemma

Does the random-root argument work with several variables?

EqualityTest works because a nonzero polynomial in one variable has few roots. Some verification equations involve several independent random values. The lecture therefore states the multivariable version of the same result. Our Fibonacci example still uses one variable; this extension is useful for other polynomial tests, including the previous midterm exercise that follows.

Consider \(h(X,Y)=XY\) in \(\mathbb F_7\). Choose \(r_1,r_2\) independently and uniformly from the seven field elements. The product is zero when \(r_1=0\) or \(r_2=0\). There are seven pairs of each kind, but \((0,0)\) belongs to both, giving \(7+7-1=13\) zero pairs out of 49.

What does degree mean for several variables?

For each term, add the exponents of its variables. The total degree of the polynomial is the largest such sum. For example, \(3X^2Y^3Z+2XY^2\) has total degree 6, because the first term has exponents \(2+3+1=6\).

Lemma 2 (Schwartz–Zippel). Let \(h(X_1,\ldots,X_m)\) be a nonzero polynomial of total degree at most \(d\) over a field. Choose each \(r_i\) independently and uniformly from a finite subset \(\Omega\) of that field. Then

\[\boxed{\Pr[h(r_1,\ldots,r_m)=0]\le\frac{d}{|\Omega|}.}\]

Notice that the bound uses the total degree, not the number of variables. We will prove why.

Proof by induction on the number of variables

For one variable, this is the root theorem we already proved. Suppose the lemma is true for \(m-1\) variables. Group the terms of \(h\) by powers of its last variable \(X_m\):

\[ h(X_1,\ldots,X_m)=\sum_{j=0}^{t}g_j(X_1,\ldots,X_{m-1})X_m^j, \qquad g_t\ne0. \]

The highest power of \(X_m\) is \(t\), so its coefficient polynomial \(g_t\) is nonzero. Because the total degree of \(h\) is at most \(d\), the total degree of \(g_t\) is at most \(d-t\).

Choose the first \(m-1\) random values. The induction hypothesis bounds the probability that \(g_t(r_1,\ldots,r_{m-1})=0\) by \((d-t)/|\Omega|\). Otherwise the leading coefficient remains nonzero. After fixing those first values, \(h\) is now a nonzero polynomial of degree \(t\) in the single remaining variable \(X_m\). The root theorem bounds the probability that a random \(r_m\) makes it zero by \(t/|\Omega|\).

Every zero of \(h\) occurs either when the leading coefficient vanishes or when it remains nonzero and the resulting one-variable polynomial has a root. Adding the two probability bounds gives

\[ \Pr[h(\mathbf r)=0]\le \frac{d-t}{|\Omega|}+\frac{t}{|\Omega|} =\frac{d}{|\Omega|}. \]

That proves the lemma for \(m\) variables, completing the induction. The essential condition is that the polynomial is nonzero; otherwise every random point would make it zero. \(\square\)

In our example \(h(X,Y)=XY\), the total degree is 2, so the bound is \(2/7=14/49\). We counted 13 actual zero pairs. The numerical example satisfies the general bound; it did not establish the bound on its own.

II · Polynomial reasoning · 2025 midterm, Problem 3(c.1)

Exam problem: can a vector hide more than one vote?

This problem is important because the arithmetic is simple but the logic must be exact. You have to prove that every invalid vector gives a nonzero polynomial—not just the invalid examples you can think of.

Previous midterm · Problem 3(c.1) · Four marks

We work in \(\mathbb Z_p\), for an odd prime \(p\). A vector \(\mathbf x=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)\) is allowed to be either all zero, or a standard basis vector \(\mathbf e_i\), which has exactly one entry equal to 1 and all others zero.

The verifier chooses \(\mathbf r=(r_1,\ldots,r_N)\) uniformly at random and makes \(\mathbf r_{\mathrm{sq}}=(r_1^2,\ldots,r_N^2)\). It accepts if

\[ \langle\mathbf x,\mathbf r\rangle^2= \langle\mathbf x,\mathbf r_{\mathrm{sq}}\rangle. \]

The angle brackets mean \(\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf r\rangle=\sum_i x_i r_i\): multiply matching entries and add. Prove perfect completeness and soundness error at most \(2/p\).

Show every step of the proof

1. An allowed vector always passes

If \(\mathbf x=\mathbf0\), both sums are zero. If \(\mathbf x=\mathbf e_j\), the left inner product picks out \(r_j\), and squaring it gives \(r_j^2\). The right inner product also picks out \(r_j^2\). So every allowed vector passes for every choice of \(\mathbf r\). That's perfect completeness.

2. What would make an invalid vector pass?

Put both sides of the acceptance equation on one side:

\[ h(\mathbf r)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^N x_i r_i\right)^2 -\sum_{i=1}^N x_i r_i^2. \]

For the fixed vector \(\mathbf x\), this is a polynomial in the random numbers \(r_i\). The verifier accepts exactly when \(h(\mathbf r)=0\). It has total degree at most 2. We still need to show it is nonzero for every invalid vector.

3. Every invalid vector falls into one of two cases

Case A: exactly one entry is nonzero. Say \(x_j=a\). Since the vector is invalid, \(a\ne1\); since it's the nonzero entry, \(a\ne0\). Then

\[ h(\mathbf r)=(ar_j)^2-ar_j^2 =a(a-1)r_j^2. \]

Neither \(a\) nor \(a-1\) is zero, so their product is nonzero in a field. Thus \(h\) is not the zero polynomial.

Case B: two or more entries are nonzero. Choose two, \(x_i\ne0\) and \(x_j\ne0\), with \(i\ne j\). Squaring the first sum creates the term \(2x_ix_jr_ir_j\). The second sum contains only squared terms \(r_k^2\), so it cannot cancel a term containing \(r_ir_j\) with two distinct indices. Since \(p\) is odd, \(2x_ix_j\ne0\). Again \(h\) is nonzero.

These cases exhaust the invalid possibilities. The all-zero vector is valid; any other invalid vector has either one nonzero entry that is not 1, or more than one nonzero entry.

4. Apply Schwartz–Zippel

For every invalid vector we've proved that \(h\) is nonzero and has total degree at most 2. Each random coordinate comes from the \(p\)-element field. So

\[ \boxed{\Pr[\text{invalid vector passes}]= \Pr[h(\mathbf r)=0]\le\frac2p.} \]

That's the soundness proof. We established the precise polynomial whose zero means acceptance, showed it can't be identically zero for an invalid vote, and then used its degree to bound false acceptance. \(\square\)

The assumption that \(p\) is odd matters. If \(p=2\), the coefficient 2 in Case B becomes zero, and that argument no longer works.

Lecture §3 · Vanishing polynomials and Theorem 2

How can 99 recurrence equations become one identity?

The verifier needs to know that the Fibonacci discrepancy \(D(i)\) is zero for every \(i=0,\ldots,98\). EqualityTest checks an identity that holds everywhere, but our recurrence is only required at these particular positions. We must first turn those 99 conditions into one identity that is true everywhere exactly when all the conditions hold.

Recall the discrepancy polynomial:

\[ D(X)=f_{\rm fib}(X+2)-f_{\rm fib}(X+1)-f_{\rm fib}(X). \]

For each required step \(i\), the recurrence is correct if and only if \(D(i)=0\). Let \(\Omega=\{0,1,\ldots,98\}\) be the set of positions where this must happen. We want a polynomial that is zero at precisely these designated points.

Build a polynomial with the required roots

For one required point \(a\), the factor \(X-a\) is zero at \(X=a\). To make a polynomial zero at every

\[ \boxed{Z_\Omega(X)=\prod_{a\in\Omega}(X-a).} \]

This is the vanishing polynomial of \(\Omega\). For the Fibonacci steps, it is \(X(X-1)\cdots(X-98)\). The symbol \(\prod\) means to multiply all the listed factors. The polynomial has degree \(|\Omega|=99\), because it is a product of 99 linear factors.

Why is this useful? If \(D(X)\) contains every factor of \(Z_\Omega(X)\), then it must be zero at every required position. The converse is also true, and proving it is what allows us to replace 99 equations by one.

Theorem 2: zeros on \(\Omega\) are equivalent to divisibility by \(Z_\Omega\)

Forward direction. Suppose \(D(X)=Z_\Omega(X)q(X)\) for some polynomial \(q\). At any \(a\in\Omega\), one factor of \(Z_\Omega(a)\) is \(a-a=0\). Therefore \(D(a)=Z_\Omega(a)q(a)=0\). Thus every required recurrence step holds.

Reverse direction. Suppose \(D(a)=0\) for every \(a\in\Omega\). By the factor theorem, each such root supplies a factor \(X-a\). Because the points are distinct, after dividing out the factor for one root, every other root remains a root of the quotient: this follows from the same cancellation argument used in Theorem 1. Repeating for all points gives

\[ D(X)=\left(\prod_{a\in\Omega}(X-a)\right)q(X) =Z_\Omega(X)q(X). \]

If \(D\) is identically zero, choose \(q=0\). Both directions are proved. Hence \(D\) is zero on \(\Omega\) if and only if it is divisible by \(Z_\Omega\). \(\square\)

Check the equivalence on a small example

Take \(\Omega=\{1,2\}\) and \(D(X)=X^3-7X+6\). Factoring gives \(D(X)=(X-1)(X-2)(X+3)\). Therefore \(Z_\Omega(X)=(X-1)(X-2)\) and the quotient is \(q(X)=X+3\). The two required zeros are present precisely because the vanishing polynomial divides \(D\).

This example is not the general proof; it simply shows how the theorem can be used to construct a quotient.

We have now achieved the crucial algebraic step. Instead of asking the verifier to check 99 separate values \(D(i)=0\), the prover can claim that there exists a polynomial \(q\) satisfying the global identity \(D(X)=Z_\Omega(X)q(X)\). The verifier can test that identity at one random point. We call the resulting protocol ZeroTest.

Lecture §3 · ZeroTest and Lemma 3

How can the verifier check the quotient identity?

The vanishing-polynomial theorem tells us that all required zeros are present exactly when \(f(X)=Z_\Omega(X)q(X)\) for some polynomial \(q\). The prover can supply a proposed quotient. Now we must check that the identity is genuine without reading every coefficient or testing every required point.

The protocol in the ideal polynomial oracle

The lecture states the test for a general polynomial \(f\) of degree at most \(d\) and a set \(\Omega\) of \(k\le d\) field points. The prover fixes \(f\) and a claimed quotient \(q\) in the oracle, with degree at most \(d\) for the quotient. The verifier chooses a uniformly random field point \(r\), requests \(f(r)\) and \(q(r)\), computes the known vanishing polynomial \(Z_\Omega(r)\), and accepts if

\[\boxed{f(r)=q(r)Z_\Omega(r).}\]

Why choose \(r\) only after

Try an honest and a dishonest quotient identity

Over \(\mathbb F_{17}\), the required zeros are at 1 and 2. Compare the honest polynomial \(f=Z_\Omega q\) with a dishonest polynomial that differs by \(X-3\). At \(r=3\), the false identity happens to pass.

Prove completeness and soundness

Lemma 3: perfect completeness and error at most \(2d/|\mathbb F|\)

Completeness. If \(f\) is zero at every point of \(\Omega\), Theorem 2 guarantees a polynomial \(q\) with \(f=Z_\Omega q\). The honest prover supplies that quotient. The identity then holds for every possible \(r\), so the verifier always accepts.

Soundness. Suppose \(f\) is not zero at every point of \(\Omega\). By Theorem 2, no polynomial quotient can make the identity true everywhere. For any fixed cheating quotient \(q\), define

\[h(X)=f(X)-q(X)Z_\Omega(X).\]

This polynomial is nonzero, because otherwise \(f=Z_\Omega q\), contradicting the false claim. We must also bound its degree. The polynomial \(f\) has degree at most \(d\), the quotient \(q\) has degree at most \(d\), and \(Z_\Omega\) has degree \(k\le d\). Therefore \(qZ_\Omega\) has degree at most \(d+k\le2d\), and so does \(h\).

The verifier accepts the false claim only when \(h(r)=0\). The root theorem says a nonzero polynomial of degree at most \(2d\) has at most \(2d\) roots. With \(r\) uniform in \(\mathbb F\),

\[\boxed{\Pr[\text{false ZeroTest acceptance}]\le\frac{2d}{|\mathbb F|}.}\]

This proves the lecture's Lemma 3. The proof succeeds because a false vanishing claim produces a fixed, nonzero, low-degree difference polynomial. \(\square\)

The bound may be uninformative if the field is too small. A cryptographic implementation uses a sufficiently large field and enforces the polynomial degree bounds; our small numerical example is only a demonstration.

ZeroTest now gives the verifier a sound way to check that a polynomial is zero at many prescribed points. We can apply it to the Fibonacci discrepancy \(D\), but the final protocol must also check the two starting values and the claimed output. Next we assemble those checks and prove that together they establish the original Fibonacci claim.

Lecture §3 · Putting the polynomial IOP together

Have we really proved the hundredth Fibonacci number?

We can now build the complete Fibonacci verification protocol. The verifier checks the starting values, the claimed final value, and one randomized polynomial identity representing every recurrence step. We will show why these checks force the claimed result, and identify what is still missing before the protocol can work with a real prover.

Let \(f_{\rm fib}(X)\) be the polynomial that the prover claims encodes the sequence at positions \(0,1,\ldots,100\). Its degree is at most 100. Let \(\Omega=\{0,1,\ldots,98\}\) be the positions where the recurrence must hold. Define

\[ D(X)=f_{\rm fib}(X+2)-f_{\rm fib}(X)-f_{\rm fib}(X+1), \qquad Z_\Omega(X)=\prod_{i=0}^{98}(X-i). \]

If the recurrence is correct at every step, the polynomial \(D\) is divisible by \(Z_\Omega\). The prover therefore places both \(f_{\rm fib}\) and a quotient polynomial \(q\) in our ideal polynomial oracle, claiming \(D=Z_\Omega q\).

What does the verifier actually ask?

First the verifier checks the two starting values and the claimed final value. In the consistent indexing used here, these requirements are

\[ f_{\rm fib}(0)=0,\qquad f_{\rm fib}(1)=1,\qquad f_{\rm fib}(100)=354\,224\,848\,179\,261\,915\,075. \]

Next it chooses one random field element \(r\). Instead of checking 99 recurrence positions, it requests exactly four evaluations: \(f_{\rm fib}(r)\), \(f_{\rm fib}(r+1)\), \(f_{\rm fib}(r+2)\) and \(q(r)\). It checks

\[ \boxed{ f_{\rm fib}(r+2)-f_{\rm fib}(r)-f_{\rm fib}(r+1) \stackrel{?}{=}q(r)\prod_{i=0}^{98}(r-i). } \]
See why the starting values force the sequence

Follow a shorter example: once two consecutive numbers are known, the recurrence fixes the next one. The same reasoning works all the way to step 100.

Why do these tests prove the original claim?

Suppose the oracle answers are honest and all the algebraic conditions hold. We know the values at 0 and 1. The recurrence at step 0 forces the next value to be \(0+1=1\). The recurrence at step 1 forces the next to be \(1+1=2\). After that each new value is forced by its two predecessors. By induction, all values at positions 0 through 100 must equal the Fibonacci sequence determined by the chosen starting conditions.

In particular, the value at position 100 cannot be invented independently: it is determined by the initial values and the recurrence. The separate final-value check makes sure it equals the public number being claimed.

Now suppose the public final-value claim is false. If an endpoint check fails, the verifier rejects directly. Otherwise, if every recurrence equation were also correct, induction would force the true Fibonacci value at position 100—contradicting the false target. Therefore at least one required recurrence equation must fail.

By Theorem 2, no quotient \(q\) can then satisfy \(D=Z_\Omega q\) as a polynomial identity. In particular, the polynomial

\[h(X)=D(X)-q(X)Z_\Omega(X)\]

is nonzero for every permitted quotient. Its degree is at most 199: the recurrence discrepancy \(D\) has degree at most 100, while \(Z_\Omega\) has degree 99 and the oracle restricts \(q\) to degree at most 100. A uniformly random \(r\) therefore satisfies \(h(r)=0\) with probability at most \(199/|\mathbb F|\), and hence at most the lecture's simpler bound

\[\boxed{\Pr[\text{false claim accepted}]\le\frac{200}{|\mathbb F|}.}\]

This probability argument applies because \(f_{\rm fib}\) and \(q\) are fixed before the verifier samples \(r\). We have established the exact link between a false computation and the root theorem: a false claim forces a fixed, nonzero polynomial of bounded degree.

So the protocol checks precisely what we needed: it ties the public result to a sequence forced by the recurrence, without reading every recurrence step. That's the reason the polynomial test can replace the long explicit computation. \(\square\)

Two problems remain

First, our polynomial oracle is imaginary. A real prover could wait for \(r\) and invent values that make the check pass. We need a commitment scheme that fixes the polynomials and authenticates their answers before the random point is chosen.

Second, there's still a cost hidden in the verifier's work. Directly evaluating \(Z_\Omega(r)\) means multiplying 99 factors. If our computation had a million steps, that would require roughly a million factors. The verifier would no longer be succinct. In PLONK we'll choose special evaluation positions, or use circuit-specific preprocessing, so the needed vanishing polynomials can be evaluated efficiently.

The first problem is a cryptographic one: how do we replace the ideal polynomial box with short authenticated evaluations? This is exactly what the next lecture section's polynomial commitment scheme is for.
