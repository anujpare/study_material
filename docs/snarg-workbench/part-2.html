II · Polynomial reasoning · Lecture §3

Fields: the rule behind the proofs

Question. The coming soundness proofs count the roots of polynomials. Under what arithmetic rules is that root count valid?

In elementary algebra, a polynomial is an expression such as \(f(X)=3X^2-7X+6\). The variable \(X\) is a placeholder: \(f(2)\) means replace every \(X\) by \(2\). The degree is the largest exponent with nonzero coefficient; here it is \(3\). A root is a value \(a\) for which \(f(a)=0\).

Cryptographic protocols usually perform the arithmetic in a finite field rather than over the real numbers. The notation \(\mathbb F_p\), for a prime \(p\), means the residue classes \(0,1,\ldots,p-1\), with addition and multiplication taken modulo \(p\). For example in \(\mathbb F_{17}\), \(15+5=3\) and \(5\cdot4=3\), because both integer results leave remainder 3 when divided by 17.

What makes this a field is that every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. For instance \(5^{-1}=7\) modulo 17 because \(5\cdot7=35\equiv1\pmod{17}\). Multiplying by an inverse permits cancellation: if \(ab=0\) in a field and \(a\ne0\), then \(b=a^{-1}ab=0\).

This is not a merely technical condition. It is the exact step that makes our coming proof work: from \((b-a)h(b)=0\) and \(b-a\ne0\), we will conclude \(h(b)=0\).

II · Polynomial reasoning · Lecture §3

One polynomial can encode many values

Question. How can one mathematical object represent an entire list of computed values without listing a separate variable for each one?

Suppose we have prescribed values \(y_0,y_1,y_2\) at the distinct points \(0,1,2\). We seek a polynomial \(f\) that takes exactly those values. This reverse process, from evaluated values to the polynomial, is called interpolation.

Start with two values, \(f(0)=2\) and \(f(1)=5\). Try a degree-one polynomial \(f(X)=aX+b\). Substitution gives \(b=2\) and \(a+b=5\), hence \(a=3\). The resulting polynomial \(f(X)=3X+2\) agrees with both values.

Three prescribed points generally require degree at most two. A useful construction that makes the first two conditions hold automatically is

\[f(X)=y_0+(y_1-y_0)X+bX(X-1).\]

At \(X=0\), both terms containing \(X\) vanish, leaving \(y_0\). At \(X=1\), the last term vanishes, leaving \(y_0+(y_1-y_0)=y_1\). Only the third condition remains. Substituting \(X=2\) gives \(y_2=2y_1-y_0+2b\), so

\[b=\frac{y_2-2y_1+y_0}{2}.\]

Division by two is valid because we choose a field of odd characteristic. This formula constructs the desired polynomial directly, without assuming that it already exists.

Interpolate three field values

Changing any prescribed value changes the unique degree-at-most-two polynomial that fits all three points.

Why is the interpolating polynomial unique?

Suppose \(f\) and \(g\) both have degree at most two and agree at \(0,1,2\). Their difference \(h=f-g\) has degree at most two and has three distinct roots. We will prove in the next lesson that any nonzero degree-two polynomial over a field has at most two roots. Therefore \(h\) must be the zero polynomial, so \(f=g\).

The construction establishes existence; the root bound establishes uniqueness. More generally, \(M\) values at \(M\) distinct field points determine exactly one polynomial of degree at most \(M-1\). \(\square\)

Notice the distinction between representing and compressing. Interpolation encodes the entire list algebraically, but representing its coefficients may still require linear space. The benefit is that a verifier can exploit algebraic identities of this polynomial without reading every encoded value.

II · Polynomial reasoning · Lecture §3, Theorem 1 / Lemma 1

EqualityTest: why one random point works

Question. Given two fixed polynomials of degree at most \(d\), can a verifier test equality without comparing all their coefficients?

Let \(f,g\in\mathbb F[X]\) with degree at most \(d\). The verifier chooses a field element \(r\) uniformly at random, queries the two polynomials at \(r\), and accepts if \(f(r)=g(r)\).

If the polynomials really are equal, their values agree at every point; honest verification therefore never fails. But if they differ, could they still agree at many points? The answer follows from a theorem about roots.

Theorem 1 · A nonzero degree-\(d\) polynomial has at most \(d\) distinct roots

First recall the polynomial remainder theorem: dividing \(h(X)\) by the linear polynomial \(X-a\) leaves the constant remainder \(h(a)\). In particular, \(h(a)=0\) implies

\[h(X)=(X-a)k(X).\]

Now assume \(a\) and \(b\) are distinct roots. Substitute \(X=b\) into the factorization: \(0=h(b)=(b-a)k(b)\). Because \(b-a\ne0\) and we are in a field, we can cancel it to conclude \(k(b)=0\). Thus after extracting the factor for one root, every other root remains a root of the quotient \(k\).

Each extracted factor reduces the degree by one. Repeating cannot extract more than \(d\) distinct factors from a nonzero degree-\(d\) polynomial: otherwise the degree would have to be at least \(d+1\), a contradiction.

Hence a nonzero polynomial of degree \(d\) has at most \(d\) distinct roots. The proof succeeds precisely because field cancellation preserves the remaining roots after each factor is extracted. \(\square\)

Lemma 1 · EqualityTest

Completeness. If \(f=g\), the equality \(f(r)=g(r)\) holds for every \(r\); the verifier accepts with probability 1.

Soundness. If \(f\ne g\), define \(h(X)=f(X)-g(X)\). Because the polynomials differ, \(h\) is nonzero. Subtraction cannot increase maximum degree, so \(\deg h\le d\). The verifier accepts the false claim exactly when \(h(r)=0\).

The root theorem proves that at most \(d\) of the \(|\mathbb F|\) uniformly likely choices of \(r\) can cause such an error:

\[\Pr_{r\leftarrow\mathbb F}[f(r)=g(r)\mid f\ne g]\le\frac{d}{|\mathbb F|}.\]

This is the desired soundness guarantee, not merely a related fact: we identified the event “false acceptance” with a root of a specific nonzero degree-bounded polynomial, then counted those roots. \(\square\)

EqualityTest: exact false-acceptance points

Compare \(f(X)=X^2+1\) with \(g(X)=f(X)+(X-1)(X-4)\) over \(\mathbb F_{17}\). Their difference has exactly two roots.

Essential condition. Both low-degree polynomials must be fixed before the verifier samples \(r\). Otherwise the prover could choose different polynomials that agree at the already-revealed point. The commitment mechanism later enforces this order.

Source: official notes, Theorem 1 and Lemma 1 (p. 5).

II · Polynomial reasoning · Lecture §3, Lemma 2

Schwartz–Zippel: several random variables

Question. If a verifier's consistency equation depends on several random field elements, what replaces the one-variable root bound?

A polynomial may use several variables. For example, \(h(X,Y,Z)=3X^2Y^3Z+2XY^2\). The total degree of a term is the sum of its exponents, so the first term has degree \(2+3+1=6\); the polynomial has total degree 6.

Lemma 2 (Schwartz–Zippel). Let \(h(X_1,\ldots,X_m)\) be a nonzero polynomial of total degree at most \(d\) over a field \(\mathbb F\). Choose \(r_1,\ldots,r_m\) independently and uniformly from a finite subset \(\Omega\subseteq\mathbb F\). Then

\[\Pr[h(r_1,\ldots,r_m)=0]\le\frac{d}{|\Omega|}.\]

Proof by induction on the number of variables

For one variable, the statement is Theorem 1: at most \(d\) of the \(|\Omega|\) choices are roots.

Assume the result for \(m-1\) variables. Collect powers of the last variable \(X_m\), writing

\[h(X_1,\ldots,X_m)=\sum_{j=0}^{t}g_j(X_1,\ldots,X_{m-1})X_m^j,\quad g_t\ne0.\]

The exponent \(t\) is the highest power of \(X_m\) that occurs. Since total degree is at most \(d\), the coefficient polynomial \(g_t\) has total degree at most \(d-t\).

First choose the values \(r_1,\ldots,r_{m-1}\). By the induction hypothesis, the probability that \(g_t(r_1,\ldots,r_{m-1})=0\) is at most \((d-t)/|\Omega|\).

On every remaining choice, \(g_t(r_1,\ldots,r_{m-1})\ne0\). Holding those coordinates fixed now makes \(h\) a nonzero one-variable polynomial in \(X_m\) of degree \(t\). The root theorem bounds the conditional chance that a uniform \(r_m\) makes it zero by \(t/|\Omega|\).

The event “\(h=0\)” lies either in the first bad case (leading coefficient vanishes) or in the second (a root of the remaining one-variable polynomial). Adding the two upper bounds gives

\[\Pr[h(\mathbf r)=0]\le\frac{d-t}{|\Omega|}+\frac{t}{|\Omega|}=\frac d{|\Omega|}.\]

This completes the induction. At every step the polynomial remains nonzero on the branch where we apply the root theorem, which is the necessary logical condition. \(\square\)

How to use it in an exam: first form the polynomial that is zero exactly when the verifier accepts, then prove it is nonzero for a false statement, then bound its total degree, and only then apply Schwartz–Zippel. Last year's common-mistakes report explicitly warns against omitting the nonzero step.

Midterm exercise: a one-hot vector test

Exam objective. Prove perfect completeness and soundness at most \(2/p\) for a random polynomial test, including the two separate reasons why a false input produces a nonzero polynomial.

2025 midterm · Problem 3(c.1) · Four marks

A vector \(\mathbf x=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)\in\mathbb Z_p^N\), for an odd prime \(p\), is valid if it is the all-zero vector or a standard basis vector \(\mathbf e_i\) with a single \(1\) and all other entries zero. Let \(\mathbf r=(r_1,\ldots,r_N)\) be uniform over \(\mathbb Z_p^N\) and define \(\mathbf r_{\rm sq}=(r_1^2,\ldots,r_N^2)\).

The verifier accepts exactly when

\[\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf r\rangle^2=\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf r_{\rm sq}\rangle\pmod p.\]

Prove perfect completeness and soundness error at most \(2/p\).

Full written-exam solution

Completeness. If \(\mathbf x=\mathbf0\), both inner products are zero, so the equality always holds. If \(\mathbf x=\mathbf e_i\), the first inner product equals \(r_i\), and the second equals \(r_i^2\); squaring the first gives exactly the second. This covers all valid inputs and all possible verifier randomness.

Soundness. For an invalid \(\mathbf x\), define the polynomial in the random coordinates

\[h(\mathbf r)=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^Nx_ir_i\Bigr)^2-\sum_{i=1}^Nx_ir_i^2.\]

It has total degree at most 2. We must prove it is nonzero. There are two exhaustive cases.

Case A: exactly one nonzero component. Let \(x_j=a\ne0\). Since the vector is invalid, \(a\ne1\). Then \(h(\mathbf r)=(a^2-a)r_j^2=a(a-1)r_j^2\). Both factors are nonzero in the field, so this is a nonzero polynomial.

Case B: at least two nonzero components. Choose \(i\ne j\) with \(x_i,x_j\ne0\). Expanding the square creates a cross-term \(2x_ix_jr_ir_j\). The other sum contains only terms \(r_k^2\), so it cannot cancel the cross-term. Because \(p\) is odd, \(2x_ix_j\ne0\). Hence \(h\ne0\).

These cases cover every invalid vector. Schwartz–Zippel now applies to a nonzero polynomial of total degree at most 2 sampled over \(\mathbb Z_p\):

\[\Pr_{\mathbf r}[V\text{ accepts invalid }\mathbf x]=\Pr_{\mathbf r}[h(\mathbf r)=0]\le\frac2p.\]

The accepted equality is exactly the event \(h=0\); nonzeroness and total degree 2 were proved for every invalid input. Therefore the claimed soundness bound follows. \(\square\)

The condition \(p\ne2\) is essential to the cross-term argument: in characteristic 2, the coefficient \(2x_ix_j\) would vanish. The original midterm works over a large prime field.

II · Polynomial reasoning · Lecture §3, Theorem 2

Vanishing polynomials

Question. EqualityTest checks that two polynomials agree everywhere. What if we only need to check an identity at certain designated positions?

Suppose we want \(f(a)=0\) for every element \(a\) of a finite set \(\Omega\). Checking every element separately would require \(|\Omega|\) evaluations. Instead construct the vanishing polynomial

\[Z_\Omega(X)=\prod_{a\in\Omega}(X-a).\]

The product symbol means one factor for each distinct \(a\in\Omega\). The resulting polynomial has degree \(|\Omega|\) and, by construction, evaluates to zero at every element of \(\Omega\).

Theorem 2 · Divisibility is equivalent to vanishing

\[\boxed{\ f(a)=0\text{ for all }a\in\Omega\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(X)=Z_\Omega(X)q(X)\text{ for some polynomial }q.\ }\]

Right to left. If \(f=Z_\Omega q\), then for each \(a\in\Omega\) one factor of \(Z_\Omega(a)\) is zero, so \(f(a)=0\). This proves that divisibility is sufficient.

Left to right. List the distinct elements as \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\). Since \(f(a_1)=0\), the factor theorem gives \(f(X)=(X-a_1)q_1(X)\). For the next root \(a_2\ne a_1\), substituting yields \(0=(a_2-a_1)q_1(a_2)\). Field cancellation implies \(q_1(a_2)=0\), so \(q_1\) also has factor \(X-a_2\). Continuing through all \(k\) distinct roots yields \(f(X)=(X-a_1)\cdots(X-a_k)q(X)=Z_\Omega(X)q(X)\).

We proved both directions. Therefore a condition at every point of \(\Omega\) is equivalent to the existence of one quotient polynomial \(q\), which is what the prover will supply. \(\square\)

A concrete factorization
\[f(X)=X^3-7X+6=(X-1)(X-2)(X+3).\]

For \(\Omega=\{1,2\}\), we obtain \(Z_\Omega=(X-1)(X-2)\) and quotient \(q=X+3\). This example illustrates the theorem; the preceding proof establishes it for any finite set of distinct field points.

Source: official notes, Theorem 2 (pp. 5–6).

II · Polynomial reasoning · Lecture §3, Lemma 3

ZeroTest: a complete proof

Question. Given the equivalence \(f=Z_\Omega q\), how can the verifier check it with one random challenge rather than calculating or receiving the whole polynomial?

Fix \(f\) of degree at most \(d\) and a set \(\Omega\) of \(k\le d\) distinct field points. The prover provides a polynomial \(q\), claiming that it is the quotient \(f/Z_\Omega\). In the ideal polynomial oracle model, the prover fixes both polynomials before the verifier selects \(r\leftarrow\mathbb F\). The verifier asks for \(f(r),q(r)\), computes \(Z_\Omega(r)\), and accepts exactly when

\[\boxed{f(r)=q(r)Z_\Omega(r).}\]
ZeroTest at a random field point

Compare an honest polynomial with a false claim whose error is nonzero except at one challenge.

Lemma 3 · Completeness and soundness

Completeness. If \(f\) really vanishes on all of \(\Omega\), Theorem 2 supplies a polynomial \(q\) with the identity \(f=Z_\Omega q\). Evaluating a true polynomial identity at any \(r\) preserves equality, so the verifier accepts with probability 1.

Soundness. Suppose the claim is false, so \(f\) does not vanish over all of \(\Omega\). Then Theorem 2 proves that \(f\) is not divisible by \(Z_\Omega\). Consequently no polynomial \(q\) can make the identity \(f=Z_\Omega q\) hold. For any cheating candidate \(q\), the difference

\[h(X)=f(X)-q(X)Z_\Omega(X)\]

is therefore nonzero. Suppose the protocol restricts \(\deg q\le d\), as in the lecture's bound. The degree of \(Z_\Omega\) is \(k\); multiplying polynomials adds their degrees, and subtracting cannot increase their maximum. Therefore

\[\deg h\le\max\{d,d+k\}\le2d.\]

False acceptance is exactly the event \(h(r)=0\). Applying Theorem 1 to this nonzero degree-at-most-\(2d\) polynomial gives

\[\Pr[\text{false acceptance}]\le\frac{2d}{|\mathbb F|}.\]

The soundness proof succeeds because Theorem 2 establishes \(h\ne0\) whenever the original claim is false, and the degree restriction bounds the number of challenges at which \(h\) could nevertheless vanish. \(\square\)

Why degree restrictions are necessary. Without a degree bound, a cheating prover could choose a high-degree \(q\) that agrees with the proposed identity at many selected points. Later, polynomial-commitment setup parameters and degree-enforcement guarantees restrict the admissible polynomials.

Where this goes. Our Fibonacci computation is a collection of recurrence equations at consecutive indices. Vanishing polynomials now let us replace those many equations by one quotient identity, and ZeroTest lets the verifier inspect it at one random point.

Source: official notes, ZeroTest and Lemma 3 (pp. 5–6); handwritten notes pp. 12–15.

II · Polynomial reasoning · Lecture §3

Proving a long Fibonacci computation

Question. We can now test a polynomial identity. Which identity expresses that every Fibonacci step was computed correctly, and how does the verifier recover the claimed last value?

We use the standard indexing \(F_0=0,F_1=1,F_{i+2}=F_i+F_{i+1}\), for which \(F_{100}=354\,224\,848\,179\,261\,915\,075\). The source notes write \(f_{\rm fib}(0)=f_{\rm fib}(1)=1\) but retain this \(F_{100}\) number; those two conditions are inconsistent. Our correction makes the calculation coherent while retaining the exact protocol method of the lecture.

Choose a prime field large enough to represent the required integers without wraparound. The prover interpolates one polynomial \(f_{\rm fib}\) through the 101 values \(F_0,\ldots,F_{100}\); it can have degree at most 100. We must enforce three kinds of facts: the two initial values, the claimed last value, and the 99 recurrence relations.

\[\begin{aligned}f_{\rm fib}(0)&=0,\quad f_{\rm fib}(1)=1,\\f_{\rm fib}(100)&=354\,224\,848\,179\,261\,915\,075,\\f_{\rm fib}(i+2)&=f_{\rm fib}(i+1)+f_{\rm fib}(i),\quad i=0,\ldots,98.\end{aligned}\]

Turn the recurrence into a ZeroTest

Define the recurrence discrepancy polynomial

\[R(X)=f_{\rm fib}(X+2)-f_{\rm fib}(X+1)-f_{\rm fib}(X).\]

For each \(i\in\Omega=\{0,\ldots,98\}\), the original recurrence equation holds if and only if \(R(i)=0\). Theorem 2 therefore says all 99 recurrence equations hold precisely when there exists a polynomial \(q\) satisfying

\[R(X)=q(X)Z_\Omega(X),\qquad Z_\Omega(X)=\prod_{i=0}^{98}(X-i).\]

The prover commits to \(f_{\rm fib}\) and \(q\) in the ideal oracle. The verifier checks three fixed evaluations for the endpoints, chooses uniform \(r\in\mathbb F\), and queries the values at \(r,r+1,r+2\) and \(q(r)\). Its last check is

\[f_{\rm fib}(r+2)-f_{\rm fib}(r+1)-f_{\rm fib}(r)=q(r)Z_\Omega(r).\]
The recurrence conditions determine the trace

Inspect the smaller computation \(F_0,\ldots,F_8\), which uses exactly the same recurrence equations.

Why these checks imply the intended statement

Suppose the endpoint checks pass and the recurrence ZeroTest identity is genuinely valid. At index zero we know \(f_{\rm fib}(0)=0\) and \(f_{\rm fib}(1)=1\). The recurrence at index zero then forces \(f_{\rm fib}(2)=1\). The recurrence at index one forces \(f_{\rm fib}(3)=2\). Continuing inductively: if \(f_{\rm fib}(i)=F_i\) and \(f_{\rm fib}(i+1)=F_{i+1}\), the recurrence forces \(f_{\rm fib}(i+2)=F_i+F_{i+1}=F_{i+2}\).

Thus every interpolated value must equal the corresponding Fibonacci value, including the value at 100. The final endpoint check then establishes the claimed number. A false computation can pass only if the randomized identity test errs; the ZeroTest root bound controls that probability.

This closes the connection between the algebra and the original goal: the quotient certifies every recurrence step, the initial conditions determine the entire sequence, and the last evaluation checks the claimed result. \(\square\)

Unresolved efficiency issue. The verifier still appears to need all 99 factors of \(Z_\Omega(r)\) to evaluate the vanishing polynomial. The lecture explicitly flags this as not succinct enough for the intended application. Later, a special roots-of-unity domain or preprocessing eliminates this expensive product.

Source: official notes, Fibonacci Poly-IOP, pp. 4–7; the indexing correction is a pedagogical erratum.
