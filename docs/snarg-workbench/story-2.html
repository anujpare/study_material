The main story · 07 of 12

How can one equation represent every Fibonacci step?

We can now represent our calculation by one polynomial, and we know why checking a false polynomial identity at a random point can work. What we still need is an identity that expresses all the additions in the calculation.

Remember the six values 0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5. Every new value after the first two must be the sum of the previous two. The four equations we need are:

At position 01 − 1 − 0 = 0Pass
At position 12 − 1 − 1 = 0Pass
At position 23 − 2 − 1 = 0Pass
At position 35 − 3 − 2 = 0Pass

We already arranged for one polynomial \(f(X)\) to hold the six values: \(f(i)\) gives the value at position \(i\). So the calculation at position \(i\) is correct precisely when \(f(i+2)-f(i+1)-f(i)=0\).

Instead of writing that expression four times, make it a new polynomial. Call it the discrepancy polynomial:

\[D(X)=f(X+2)-f(X+1)-f(X).\]

It's called a discrepancy because its value measures the failure of the addition rule. If the addition at position 3 is wrong by 3, then \(D(3)=3\). For the honest trace, \(D(0)=D(1)=D(2)=D(3)=0\).

Make the four required zeros into one condition

The four positions where we need the rule to hold form the set \(\Omega=\{0,1,2,3\}\). We can construct a polynomial that is zero at exactly these four field elements:

\[Z(X)=X(X-1)(X-2)(X-3).\]

At \(X=2\), for example, the factor \(X-2\) is zero, making the whole product zero. We call \(Z\) the vanishing polynomial for \(\Omega\).

Here's the key fact: \(D\) is zero at every point of \(\Omega\) if and only if it is divisible by \(Z\). “Divisible” means that some polynomial \(q\) exists for which

\[\boxed{D(X)=Z(X)q(X).}\]

Why does divisibility mean that all four additions work?

One direction is immediate. If \(D=Zq\), then at each required position \(i\in\Omega\), we have \(Z(i)=0\), so \(D(i)=0\). Every recurrence equation holds.

For the other direction, suppose every recurrence equation holds. Then 0 is a root of \(D\), so \(X\) is a factor. Since 1 is also a root, the remaining quotient must have factor \(X-1\): we can cancel the nonzero factor \(1-0\) when substituting 1. Repeat the same argument at 2 and 3. We obtain all four factors \(X(X-1)(X-2)(X-3)\) dividing \(D\), hence \(D=Zq\). If \(D\) is the zero polynomial, use \(q=0\).

We've proved both directions. The four separate additions are correct precisely when there is a quotient \(q\) making the single polynomial identity \(D=Zq\) true. \(\square\)

Recall that the interpolated trace polynomial \(f\) has degree at most 5. Substituting \(X+1\) or \(X+2\) doesn't increase its degree, so \(D\) has degree at most 5. Since \(Z\) has degree 4, an honest nonzero quotient \(q\) needs degree at most 1.

That's our new representation of the computation. The prover doesn't have to convince us separately about each addition; it can claim one polynomial identity. But it could still lie about the quotient.

Could a fake quotient repair a wrong addition?

At each required step, \(Z(i)=0\). Try the true and false traces and see what that forces \(D(i)\) to equal.

We now have a single identity equivalent to all four checks. We need a way to test it cheaply without reading every polynomial coefficient.

What did we just achieve?

Four recurrence equations became one identity about three polynomials: the committed trace \(f\), the derived discrepancy \(D\), and the quotient \(q\).

We already proved that one random evaluation can test a fixed polynomial identity. Let's apply that result to this identity—and see exactly what happens when the server lies about the final value.

The main story · 08 of 12

Can one random field element expose the false result?

We've reduced all four additions to the identity \(D(X)=Z(X)q(X)\). So choose a random number \(r\) and check whether the two sides agree at that point. This is the protocol called ZeroTest.

In our example we work in \(\mathbb F_{101}\), so the verifier chooses \(r\) uniformly from the 101 field elements 0 through 100. The prover must fix its polynomials \(f\) and \(q\) before learning \(r\). The verifier then asks for their values at the required points and checks

\[\boxed{f(r+2)-f(r+1)-f(r)\ \stackrel{?}{=}\ Z(r)q(r).}\]

The left side is \(D(r)\). The verifier can calculate the four factors of \(Z(r)=r(r-1)(r-2)(r-3)\) itself. The question mark means that it tests equality; it doesn't assume equality beforehand.

Try the same lie that fooled our random step check

The server again claims 0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 8 instead of 0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5. Interpolation produces a different trace polynomial \(f_{\rm false}\) matching that incorrect list. It has the same allowed degree, at most 5, so degree restrictions alone do not catch the lie.

Divide its discrepancy polynomial \(D_{\rm false}\) by \(Z\). Ordinary polynomial division gives a quotient \(q_{\rm false}\) and a remainder \(h\) of degree less than 4:

\[D_{\rm false}(X)=Z(X)q_{\rm false}(X)+h(X).\]

What values must the remainder take? At the four required positions, \(Z(i)=0\), so \(h(i)=D_{\rm false}(i)\). The first three additions are correct, so \(h(0)=h(1)=h(2)=0\). The last addition is wrong by 3, so \(h(3)=3\).

There is only one polynomial of degree at most 3 with those four values. It is

\[\boxed{h(X)=\tfrac12 X(X-1)(X-2)\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_{101}.}\]

You can check the formula: it is zero at 0, 1 and 2. At 3 it equals \((1/2)\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1=3\). Since two different degree-at-most-three polynomials cannot agree at four points, this is indeed the complete remainder.

One calculation, two claimed endings

Choose an honest or false final value, then change the verifier's random field point \(r\). Both sides of the ZeroTest equation are calculated in \(\mathbb F_{101}\).

Notice the contrast. Sampling one of the four original recurrence steps missed the same lie with probability \(3/4\). In this particular polynomial test the dishonest remainder has only three roots in a field of 101 elements, so the false claim passes for just \(3/101\) of the possible random challenges.

Why does the method work for any false trace of the permitted degree?

First consider an honest calculation. Every recurrence discrepancy is zero, so the divisibility theorem gives \(D=Zq\). The verifier's equation is a true identity and accepts every random \(r\). This is perfect completeness.

Now assume the public claim is false, with fixed initial values 0, 1 and a claimed last value that the recurrence does not produce. Those initial values and the recurrence uniquely determine every following value, so at least one required recurrence equation must fail. Hence for any claimed quotient \(q\), the polynomial

\[h(X)=D(X)-Z(X)q(X)\]

cannot be the zero polynomial: at a failed step \(i\in\Omega\), \(Z(i)=0\) but \(D(i)\ne0\), so \(h(i)\ne0\).

The degree of \(D\) is at most 5; if the protocol restricts \(\deg q\le1\), then \(\deg(Zq)\le4+1=5\). Thus \(\deg h\le5\). Our root theorem says that at most five of the 101 random challenges can make this nonzero \(h\) vanish:

\[\boxed{\Pr[\text{false acceptance}]\le 5/101.}\]

The specific lie ending in 8 has only three bad challenges and therefore gets the stronger exact value \(3/101\). The general bound covers any false trace with the stated degree restrictions.

We've now proved a complete statement about the computation: if its initial values, final claim and polynomial identity pass, then—except for the bounded random-test error—the entire recurrence is correct. The reason is not magic sampling; it's that a false trace forces a fixed nonzero low-degree polynomial. \(\square\)

Optional: inspect the exact quotient polynomials

For the honest six-value list, interpolating over \(\mathbb F_{101}\) and dividing its recurrence discrepancy by \(Z(X)=X(X-1)(X-2)(X-3)\) gives the quotient \(q(X)=51+21X\), with zero remainder.

For the list ending in 8, the quotient is \(q_{\rm false}(X)=79+74X\) and the remainder is \(h(X)=51X(X-1)(X-2)\). The number 51 is the inverse of 2 modulo 101, so this is precisely the polynomial derived above. Substitution at any field element verifies the identity \(D_{\rm false}=Zq_{\rm false}+h\).

These explicit coefficients are not an extra assumption. They are what ordinary interpolation and polynomial division produce from the two listed traces.

What is still imaginary?

Our proof assumes the prover has already fixed \(f\) and \(q\), and that the verifier can ask for their values without receiving the entire polynomials. So far we've treated this as a trusted polynomial box.

If the prover could invent its polynomial answers after seeing \(r\), it could make this one equation pass. We must now replace the imaginary box with a cryptographic commitment.

Lecture §4 · Polynomial Commitments

How can a verifier trust a polynomial evaluation?

Our Fibonacci proof worked in an imaginary box. The prover fixed its trace polynomial \(f\) and quotient \(q\), the verifier picked a random point \(r\), and the box returned the values that actually belonged to those fixed polynomials. A real dishonest prover could simply invent the answers after seeing \(r\). How do we stop that?

We solved a similar problem with the Merkle tree. There the commitment fixed a long list of bits, and the verifier authenticated selected entries. Now the fixed object is a polynomial, and the query asks for its value at an arbitrary field point. The cryptographic tool we need is a polynomial commitment scheme.

Four algorithms, each doing a different job

Let's follow one polynomial \(f(X)\), of degree at most \(d\), from the moment we prepare the system until a random evaluation is checked. The lecture calls the security parameter \(\lambda\), and the public setup information \(pp\).

AlgorithmWhy we need it
\(\operatorname{Setup}(d,\lambda)\to pp\)Produce the public parameters for the permitted degree \(d\) and security level \(\lambda\).
\(\operatorname{Commit}(pp,f)\to c\)The prover fixes \(f\) and sends the short commitment \(c\), before learning the verifier's query.
\(\operatorname{Open}(pp,f,z)\to\pi_z\)Once the verifier chooses point \(z\), the prover supplies the value \(y=f(z)\) and an opening proof \(\pi_z\).
\(\operatorname{Verify}(pp,c,z,y,\pi_z)\to\{0,1\}\)The verifier checks whether the claimed evaluation is authenticated by the earlier commitment.

Here \(c\) is the commitment, while \(\pi_z\) is the proof for one evaluation; they are different objects. The verifier's question \(z\) can be any field point the scheme supports, not just one of the original Fibonacci indices.

What guarantees does the scheme owe us?

Correctness: if the prover commits to \(f\) and honestly opens the true value \(f(z)\), the verification algorithm accepts. We can express that promise as

\[\Pr[\operatorname{Verify}(pp,c,z,f(z),\pi_z)=1]=1\]

when \(pp,c,\pi_z\) were generated honestly by the algorithms above.

Evaluation binding: a dishonest prover must not be able to produce one commitment \(c\), one point \(z\), two different values \(y\ne y'\), and valid proofs for both. Otherwise it could decide which answer to give after

\[\begin{gathered} \operatorname{Verify}(pp,c,z,y,\pi)=1,\\ \operatorname{Verify}(pp,c,z,y',\pi')=1,\quad y\ne y' \end{gathered}\quad\text{should be infeasible.}\]

The lecture requires this to happen with at most negligible probability for every polynomial-time attacker. “Negligible” means smaller than the reciprocal of any fixed polynomial in \(\lambda\), once \(\lambda\) is large enough.

How does this help our Fibonacci test?

The prover first commits to both \(f\) and \(q\), making \(c_f,c_q\). Only then does the verifier choose a random \(r\). The verifier will need authenticated evaluations \(f(r)\), \(f(r+1)\), \(f(r+2)\), and \(q(r)\), as well as the fixed endpoints.

Correctness makes those honest openings verifiable. Evaluation binding prevents conflicting values at the same committed point. To implement the full low-degree polynomial IOP, the commitment scheme must also provide the relevant degree enforcement and consistency needed across all queried points. The lecture defines this interface but does not specify or prove a concrete construction.

We now know what must replace the ideal box: short authenticated evaluations tied to a polynomial fixed before the random challenge. This supplies the cryptographic side of the ZeroTest blueprint, subject to the actual commitment scheme's security guarantees. \(\square\)

A polynomial commitment doesn't automatically hide its polynomial. Binding and zero knowledge answer different questions. Our lecture is building a SNARG first, not yet a zero-knowledge SNARG.

Lecture §4 · From polynomial IOP to SNARG

Can the verifier's question be reconstructed afterward?

Our polynomial proof is almost what we wanted: it authenticates a few polynomial values and detects false identities with a small error bound. The remaining problem is that the verifier must send a fresh random challenge \(r\) to the prover. Anyone who wants to check the proof later would need a new conversation.

Recall the order of the interactive protocol. First the prover commits to \(f\) and \(q\), sending \(c_f,c_q\). Next the verifier chooses \(r\) uniformly from the field. Finally the prover opens the needed evaluations. The order is essential: the polynomials are already fixed when the verifier's question appears.

Follow the three stages

Switch between a random challenge sent by the verifier and a hash-derived challenge reconstructed from the proof.

How can we remove the middle message?

Imagine replacing the verifier's randomly sent \(r\) with a value that both parties compute from the already fixed commitments and the public statement. Let \(H\) be a hash modeled as a random oracle, with output interpreted as a field element. Then set

\[r=H(\operatorname{Encode}(\text{statement},c_f,c_q,\text{parameters})).\]

The prover fixes and publishes \(c_f,c_q\), computes this \(r\), then supplies the evaluation values and their opening proofs. The verifier recomputes the same \(r\), verifies the commitment openings, checks the initial and claimed final values, and performs ZeroTest at that challenge.

There is no need to send \(r\) separately, because the verifier can reproduce it. The proof is now one transferable string containing the commitments and authenticated evaluations. This is the Fiat–Shamir transformation.

What does the transformation prove, and what doesn't it prove?

Completeness survives

Both sides calculate the same \(r\) from the same public data. An honest prover opens exactly the polynomials it committed to, and the verifier applies exactly the original identities. Therefore the algebraic completeness guarantee remains true.

The protocol no longer needs an interactive challenge while honest verification still works. \(\square\)

For soundness, we must be more careful. A cheating prover may try many first messages until a hash yields a favorable challenge. A suitable random-oracle security proof must bound that possibility; simply choosing a deterministic hash is not, by itself, a soundness proof. The small fields in our toy calculations are for understanding the algebra, not cryptographic security.

The course notes observe that this structure resembles a three-message Sigma protocol. Schnorr's protocol, from the earlier zero-knowledge homework, is a simple example: the prover fixes a first message, the verifier sends a random challenge, and the prover returns a response. Fiat–Shamir replaces that middle challenge in the same way.

What if the calculation contains more than one kind of step?

Our Fibonacci example was convenient because every step used the same rule. But an ordinary program might add in one place, multiply in another, and reuse an earlier result later. We need one common description of those different steps.

An arithmetic circuit is a collection of small operations called gates. Each gate takes two values and produces either their sum or their product. Wires connect the output of one gate to the inputs of later gates. This is the same idea as breaking an enormous computation into tiny steps, but now each step can use a different operation.

The lecture gives this a precise form. An arithmetic circuit \(C\) takes field elements as inputs and applies only binary addition or multiplication gates. We separate its inputs into a public statement \(\mathbf x\in\mathbb F^m\), known to the verifier, and a private witness \(\mathbf w\in\mathbb F^n\), known to the prover. The general statement to prove is that some witness makes the circuit output zero:

\[L=\{\mathbf x\in\mathbb F^m:\exists\,\mathbf w\in\mathbb F^n,\ C(\mathbf x,\mathbf w)=0\}.\]

Remember that \(\exists\) means “there exists.” This is the same statement-and-witness idea from the beginning of the lecture, now expressed for a circuit: the public claim is true if at least one private input makes the output zero.

To make the idea visible, use the lecture's small example. The public inputs are \(x_1=2,x_2=4\), and the prover has private input \(w_1=3\). The computation is

\[t=(x_1x_2)(x_2+w_1).\]

The first gate multiplies 2 and 4, making 8. The second adds 4 and 3, making 7. The third multiplies 8 and 7, making 56.

For this example the public claim is that the result is 56. If the formal language requires a zero output, that's equivalent to checking \(t-56=0\); we can append a checking gate in a larger circuit. Why do we need to record the wires as well as the calculations?

Look at gate 2. It must receive the actual output of gate 0 on its left wire and of gate 1 on its right wire. It isn't enough for gate 2 to perform some correct multiplication using numbers of its own choosing. This is the difference between verifying isolated arithmetic and verifying the whole circuit.

The proposed input and output values at every gate are collectively called the trace. A false trace can satisfy some constraints while violating others. We will separate the possible mistakes and see why each needs its own check.

What do we need to prove?

Our circuit has a public structure and simple gate rules. But the prover's list of gate values may still be dishonest.

We need to encode the entire trace as a polynomial, just as we did for Fibonacci, before we can use random polynomial checks.

Lecture §5.1 · From Circuits to Polynomials

How can one polynomial hold a whole circuit execution?

The circuit breaks a computation into tiny additions and multiplications. But our efficient polynomial tests don't read a table of gate values. They ask for evaluations of one fixed polynomial. We need to encode the entire proposed circuit trace in that form.

The lecture's three-gate example has nine gate entries: three values for each gate's left input, right input and output. It also has three original inputs: public \(x_1=2,x_2=4\), and private \(w_1=3\). That's twelve numbers altogether.

Let \(|C|\) denote the number of gates, and \(|I|\) the number of original inputs. The number of trace positions is

\[d=3|C|+|I|.\]

For our example, \(|C|=3\) and \(|I|=3\), so \(d=3\cdot3+3=12\). This is where the number 12 comes from; we didn't choose it arbitrarily.

Choose twelve different points to hold the values

Interpolation says that twelve prescribed values at twelve distinct field points determine a unique polynomial of degree at most 11. We could choose arbitrary distinct points, but the lecture chooses the powers of a number \(\omega\) of multiplicative order \(d\):

\[\Omega=\{1,\omega,\omega^2,\ldots,\omega^{d-1}\},\quad\omega^d=1.\]

“Order \(d\)” means \(\omega^d=1\) and no smaller positive exponent gives 1. That's important because it ensures the first \(d\) powers are different, as interpolation requires. Just having \(\omega^d=1\) without exact order \(d\) would allow repeated points.

For a concrete twelve-point example, we use \(\mathbb F_{97}\) and \(\omega=6\). Here \(6^{12}\equiv1\pmod{97}\), while \(6^6\equiv-1\ne1\) and \(6^4\equiv35\ne1\). Every proper divisor of 12 divides 6 or 4, so the order cannot be smaller than 12.

Walk through all twelve positions

The values \(6^j\bmod97\) are the twelve distinct field points used to store the trace.

Where does each value go?

The prover now interpolates a polynomial called \(f_{\rm trace}(X)\). It must put each original input at a negative-power position and each gate's three values at consecutive positive-power positions. Negative powers are valid here because \(\omega^{-1}=\omega^{11}\), \(\omega^{-2}=\omega^{10}\), and so on, since \(\omega^{12}=1\).

Original dataPolynomial positionsValues
Original inputs\(\omega^{-1},\omega^{-2},\omega^{-3}\)2, 4, 3
Gate 0 · multiply\(\omega^0,\omega^1,\omega^2\)2, 4, 8
Gate 1 · add\(\omega^3,\omega^4,\omega^5\)4, 3, 7
Gate 2 · multiply\(\omega^6,\omega^7,\omega^8\)8, 7, 56

The pattern for any gate numbered \(l=0,1,\ldots,|C|-1\) is

\[\begin{aligned} f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{3l})&=\text{left input of gate }l,\\ f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{3l+1})&=\text{right input of gate }l,\\ f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{3l+2})&=\text{output of gate }l. \end{aligned}\]

For each original input number \(j=1,\ldots,|I|\), the corresponding value is stored at \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{-j})\).

Does such a polynomial exist? Is it unique?

We've specified exactly twelve values at twelve distinct points. Interpolation constructs a polynomial of degree at most 11 matching them. If two different polynomials of degree at most 11 both matched all twelve values, their difference would be nonzero, have degree at most 11, and have twelve distinct roots. That contradicts the root bound we proved earlier.

Therefore exactly one degree-at-most-11 trace polynomial represents these twelve proposed values. The lecture sometimes uses the looser degree bound \(d=12\) in its definitions; degree 11 suffices for this interpolation. \(\square\)

But a mathematical representation is not a proof of correctness. A dishonest prover can interpolate a fake table as easily as a real one. What matters next is identifying which relations among these values must hold for the table to be an execution of the requested circuit.

What was achieved?

The entire computation trace now lives in one polynomial. A verifier can refer to any input, gate input or gate output by a corresponding field evaluation.

What should the verifier check to ensure the trace is the right calculation? The lecture lists four conditions. We'll derive them from this precise encoding.

The main story · 11 of 12

What would a cheating circuit trace have to get past?

The prover has given us a polynomial representing twelve claimed values. But interpolation would work for any twelve values. What distinguishes a real circuit execution from a fabricated one? We must check exactly the requirements that make the table an execution of our public circuit.

For our three-gate example, there are four kinds of requirement. Each catches a different way to cheat.

1 · Public inputDid the prover use the numbers 2 and 4 that the verifier supplied?
2 · Final resultDoes the circuit output equal the publicly claimed target 56?
3 · Gate arithmeticDoes each addition or multiplication give the result claimed in its own row?
4 · WiringWhen one gate feeds another, is the same value used at both ends of that wire?
Try giving the verifier a false trace

The simulator checks all four requirements separately in \(\mathbb F_{97}\). In the “wrong wiring” example, every gate is locally correct and the final result is 56—only the wire connections reveal the lie.

How do those four requirements become polynomial tests?

The lecture arranges the original input values at the positions \(\omega^{-1},\omega^{-2},\omega^{-3}\). For gate \(l\), it puts its left input, right input and output at the three positions \(\omega^{3l},\omega^{3l+1},\omega^{3l+2}\). Here \(l=0,1,2\); negative powers wrap around because \(\omega^{12}=1\).

Public input: the verifier knows that \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{-1})=2\) and \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{-2})=4\). The third original input is private and is not required to equal a public value. How can we check the two known values together? Interpolate a polynomial \(f_{\rm in}\) through the public inputs: \(f_{\rm in}(\omega^{-1})=2\) and \(f_{\rm in}(\omega^{-2})=4\). Then the difference \(g(X)=f_{\rm trace}(X)-f_{\rm in}(X)\) must vanish at both public-input positions.

That is equivalent to saying that \(g\) has the factor \((X-\omega^{-1})(X-\omega^{-2})\). We proved the equivalence in the Fibonacci lesson, so a suitable quotient and a randomized ZeroTest check can enforce the public inputs without opening every trace value.

Final result: the last gate's output is at \(\omega^{3\cdot2+2}=\omega^8\). For this public target, the verifier requires \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega^8)=56\). In the strict zero-output language, the analogous condition requires the last gate's output to equal 0 after including a target-checking gate.

Gate arithmetic: let \(L,R,O\) denote one gate's left, right and output values. Let \(s=1\) when the gate adds, and \(s=0\) when it multiplies. The same equation handles either gate:

\[\boxed{s(L+R)+(1-s)LR-O=0.}\]

Substitute \(s=1\): we obtain \(L+R-O=0\), the addition rule. Substitute \(s=0\): we obtain \(LR-O=0\), the multiplication rule. So the equation really is equivalent to the chosen gate's arithmetic, not merely related to it.

The selector belongs to the public circuit: gate 0 multiplies, gate 1 adds and gate 2 multiplies. Interpolate a selector polynomial \(s(X)=f_{\rm sel}(X)\) that takes the values 0, 1 and 0 at the three gate-start points \(1,\omega^3,\omega^6\).

At a gate-start point \(X=\omega^{3l}\), our trace encoding makes \(f_{\rm trace}(X)\) the left input, \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega X)\) the right input and \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega^2X)\) the output. Substitute those three expressions, together with \(s(X)\), into the one-gate equation. We obtain one polynomial:

\[ \begin{aligned} G(X)={}&s(X)\bigl(f_{\rm trace}(X)+f_{\rm trace}(\omega X)\bigr)\\ &+\bigl(1-s(X)\bigr)f_{\rm trace}(X)f_{\rm trace}(\omega X)\\ &-f_{\rm trace}(\omega^2X). \end{aligned} \]

Now the connection is exact: at \(X=\omega^{3l}\), the expression \(G(X)=0\) is just the required arithmetic equation for gate \(l\). Therefore every gate is correct if and only if \(G\) vanishes at all three points \(1,\omega^3,\omega^6\). The vanishing-polynomial theorem turns those three conditions into a quotient identity, and ZeroTest checks that identity at a random field point. The polynomial degrees must be bounded before that soundness argument applies.

Wiring: gate 0's output must equal gate 2's left input, and gate 1's output must equal gate 2's right input:

\[ f_{\rm trace}(\omega^2)=f_{\rm trace}(\omega^6),\qquad f_{\rm trace}(\omega^5)=f_{\rm trace}(\omega^7). \]

In the “wrong wiring” example above, the prover puts 9 and 17 into gate 2. The multiplication is correct modulo 97, because \(9\cdot17=153\equiv56\). It even produces the expected public result. But gate 0 produced 8, not 9, and gate 1 produced 7, not 17. The wiring equalities fail. That is why the gate equations alone cannot prove a circuit executed correctly.

Represent all wire copies with the rotation polynomial

Those two wiring equations are only part of the story. The original inputs also reappear inside gates: for example, the public value \(x_2=4\) is stored at \(\omega^{-2}\), gate 0's right input at \(\omega^1\), and gate 1's left input at \(\omega^3\). All three positions must contain the same number.

For any logical wire, list the places where it appears and arrange those places into a cycle. For the copies of \(x_2\), that might be \(\omega^{-2}\to\omega^1\to\omega^3\to\omega^{-2}\). Define a map that sends each position to the next place in its cycle. The lecture calls its polynomial representation \(f_{\rm rot}\), the rotation polynomial.

\[f_{\rm trace}(a)=f_{\rm trace}(f_{\rm rot}(a))\qquad\text{for every }a\in\Omega.\]

Why do these equalities force consistent wiring?

Take one wire whose occurrences form a cycle \(a_1\to a_2\to\cdots\to a_k\to a_1\). The displayed relation says the values at \(a_1\) and \(a_2\) are equal, then those at \(a_2\) and \(a_3\) are equal, and so on. By transitivity, every occurrence has exactly the same value.

Conversely, if every occurrence of a wire already has one common value, every equality in its cycle holds. Thus this family of equalities is equivalent to correct wire copying throughout the trace.

The rotation constraints close the loophole that gate equations leave open: the prover cannot feed a gate a new value in place of the value supplied by its actual wire. \(\square\)

The map depends only on the circuit wiring, not on the private witness. Since its values are prescribed at the twelve distinct domain points, it too can be represented by a polynomial using interpolation. The circuit designer can compute and commit to it once, in preprocessing. The lecturer sketches a polynomial IOP for this check but does not provide the full protocol or its security proof; we must not invent one.

Why was the roots-of-unity domain useful?

The polynomial that vanishes at all twelve powers of \(\omega\) is especially simple:

\[\prod_{j=0}^{11}(X-\omega^j)=X^{12}-1.\]

To see why, both sides are monic degree-12 polynomials with the same twelve distinct roots. Their difference has degree at most 11 but twelve roots, which our root theorem says is impossible unless the difference is identically zero. So they are the same polynomial. Computing \(r^{12}-1\) by repeated squaring uses only a few field multiplications, unlike multiplying twelve separate factors.

For arbitrary subsets of the domain, such as the public-input positions or gate-start positions, that simple identity does not automatically apply. The full PLONK construction uses further polynomial structure and preprocessing.

Where does this leave us?

We now have four precise sets of constraints. If they all hold, the trace uses the public inputs, performs each required gate operation, connects its wires consistently and reaches the claimed output.

We still need the final logical step: do those four conditions really force the existence of a satisfying computation? Let's prove that before claiming the protocol works.

How do we turn these checks into the final SNARG?

We now know what a valid circuit trace must satisfy. The final construction has one more practical question: who computes the large public circuit polynomials, who computes the witness-dependent trace, and what does the verifier actually receive? The lecture separates these jobs so the prover can do expensive work without making every verifier repeat it.

1. Universal setup

First run the polynomial commitment scheme's \(\operatorname{Setup}(d,\lambda)\to pp\). The parameter \(d\) bounds the polynomial degrees, and \(\lambda\) specifies the security level. The resulting public parameters \(pp\) are universal: within the permitted size bound, they do not depend on one particular circuit.

2. Circuit-specific preprocessing

Next take the public circuit \(C\). Its gate types and wiring are known before anyone chooses a private witness. From that fixed structure compute the selector polynomial \(f_{\rm sel}\) and rotation polynomial \(f_{\rm rot}\), then commit to both.

This may take \(O(|C|)\) work, where \(|C|\) is the number of gates. But it is done once for the circuit, not each time a new input is proved. The commitments become part of the circuit's public verification information. This is why the verifier does not have to construct the large selector and rotation polynomials from scratch.

3. The prover performs the calculation

For public input \(\mathbf x\) and its private witness \(\mathbf w\), the prover runs the circuit and records every wire value. It then interpolates the trace polynomial \(f_{\rm trace}\) and commits to it. This part depends on the actual computation and cannot be reused from an earlier proof with different inputs.

The verifier must check four things. Here they are in the same order as §6 of the original lecture:

OutputQuery \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{3|C|-1})\) and require zero for the formal zero-output circuit. In our original three-gate illustration the value was 56, so proving target 56 requires the added target-check gate.
Public inputsBuild the publicly known polynomial \(f_{\rm in}\). The prover commits to \(f_{\rm trace}-f_{\rm in}\) and to its quotient for a ZeroTest over the designated public-input positions.
Gate equationsProve that the gate polynomial \(G(X)\), built from \(f_{\rm sel}\) and \(f_{\rm trace}\), vanishes at every gate-start point. The prover supplies its quotient, and the verifier checks authenticated evaluations at random points.
Wire copiesUse the committed \(f_{\rm rot}\) in another polynomial IOP to enforce consistent values at all occurrences of each logical wire.

Why are the input polynomials consistent?

There is a subtle point in the public-input test. A cheater might commit to a convenient polynomial \(g(X)\) that vanishes at the right positions but is not actually the difference \(f_{\rm trace}-f_{\rm in}\). That would prove nothing about the real trace.

So the verifier also checks that the committed auxiliary polynomial really corresponds to that difference. It queries both at a random point \(r\), calculates \(f_{\rm in}(r)\) locally from public inputs, and tests

\[f_{\rm trace}(r)-g(r)=f_{\rm in}(r).\]

Because these polynomials were fixed before \(r\) was chosen, EqualityTest bounds the chance that a false consistency identity passes. Only after that check does ZeroTest on \(g\) establish the real public-input equality. This is why the lecture explicitly mentions consistency between the oracles rather than simply testing \(g\) in isolation.

4. Make the verifier's questions non-interactive

All of the polynomial IOP's random queries can be derived from the public statement and the commitments by Fiat–Shamir, under the appropriate random-oracle security conditions. The prover publishes one proof string containing commitments, claimed evaluations and their opening proofs. Anyone with the public verification information can recompute the challenges and check it.

Why does the construction prove the right language?

If all four constraint families are satisfied, the public inputs agree with the statement, every gate performs its prescribed operation, shared wire values agree, and the final output is zero. We can therefore read the trace as a real execution of \(C(\mathbf x,\mathbf w)\) with a satisfying witness. The random polynomial tests are meant to detect a false constraint identity with their degree-based soundness bounds.

That connects the proof data back to the actual statement \(C(\mathbf x,\mathbf w)=0\). The commitments and Fiat–Shamir are what let us enforce those algebraic conditions succinctly and non-interactively, subject to their cryptographic guarantees. \(\square\)

The lecture's goal is a proof of length \(O(\lambda,\operatorname{polylog}|C|)\), with verifier time \(O(\lambda,|\mathbf x|,\operatorname{polylog}|C|)\), where the shorthand describes growth with the security level, public input and circuit size. The proof is short even though the prover's computation may be large. The verifier can spend time reading the public input; it should not have to evaluate every gate.

The main story · 12 of 12

Have we actually proved the computation was correct?

We began with a server announcing a result and a client who didn't want to repeat a long calculation. We've developed several mathematical tools along the way. Now there is just one thing left to establish: that they genuinely answer the original question.

Suppose every circuit constraint really holds

Start at the original input wires. The public-input checks guarantee the values supplied by the verifier have not been changed. The remaining input wires specify a candidate private witness.

Next follow the circuit in the order the gates are evaluated. At each gate, the input wires have definite values because the wiring constraints force every occurrence of a logical wire to agree. The gate equation guarantees that the output is exactly the sum or product prescribed by the public circuit. That output becomes the value seen by all later gates connected to it.

Continue gate by gate. Because each operation and each connection is correct, we aren't just looking at a collection of plausible numbers. We have reconstructed a genuine evaluation of the specified circuit on the fixed public inputs and some witness.

Finally, the output check says this genuine circuit evaluation reaches the claimed result. Equivalently, after adding a final comparison with the public target, the circuit outputs zero. So a witness satisfying the public claim really exists.

The conclusion of the circuit argument

Correct public inputs and consistent wire values, together with correct arithmetic at every gate, force the trace to equal an honest circuit execution. The output constraint then forces that execution to satisfy the public claim.

Therefore a trace satisfying all four kinds of constraints is a valid witness for the circuit statement. This is the conclusion the reduction needed; without it, checking a few related equations would not amount to proving the computation. \(\square\)

But the verifier didn't check every constraint one by one

That's where the earlier polynomial reasoning is used. Interpolation puts the trace into polynomials. Vanishing-polynomial identities express constraints at many prescribed points. A random equality test can check a fixed low-degree identity with a provable error bound, because a nonzero polynomial has few roots.

Polynomial commitments authenticate the queried evaluations without sending the whole trace. Fiat–Shamir can replace the interactive random challenges with hash-derived ones, given a suitable random-oracle security argument.

The intended result is a succinct non-interactive argument: a proof whose size and verification cost grow slowly with the large computation, rather than requiring the verifier to rerun it. The prover still does the expensive computation; it is the verifier whose work we are trying to save.

Now that we know what is being shortened, we can read the formal size requirement without guessing. Let \(|w|\) be the length in bits of the long witness, \(|x|\) the length of the public statement, \(|\pi|\) the length of the new proof, and \(T_V\) the verifier's running time. The parameter \(\lambda\) controls cryptographic security. The lecture's succinctness targets are

\[ |\pi|=O\bigl(\lambda,\operatorname{polylog}(|x|,|w|)\bigr), \qquad T_V=O\bigl(\lambda,|x|,\operatorname{polylog}(|w|)\bigr). \]

Here \(\operatorname{polylog}(n)\) means a fixed power of a logarithm, and big-\(O\) describes asymptotic growth. These expressions say the proof and verifier should not grow linearly with the entire witness; the verifier may still need time to read the public statement. They are goals for suitable schemes, not size bounds we established just by checking our six-value example.

Exactly what has and hasn't been established

The lecture gives the polynomial-IOP blueprint, the root and ZeroTest soundness arguments, and the circuit constraints. It does not fully construct and prove every cryptographic ingredient: the precise commitment scheme, degree enforcement, Fiat–Shamir reduction and detailed wiring IOP require additional arguments. We should not claim a complete end-to-end security theorem from the abbreviated lecture alone.

We also haven't automatically obtained zero knowledge. A SNARG can convince the verifier without necessarily hiding the witness. Making the proof zero-knowledge requires further hiding and randomization steps.

Return to the first question

Why couldn't we just sample the ordinary calculation? A false answer could hide in one step among many.

What changed? We encoded the evidence so a few random checks have a mathematical soundness guarantee, and used commitments so a cheater cannot change the evidence after seeing the random question.

What makes this a proof rather than a collection of observations? We showed that the polynomial constraints are equivalent to valid computation steps; that passing every circuit constraint implies an actual satisfying execution; and that a false low-degree identity has only a bounded chance of passing a random test.

There are now two routes visible in the lecture: PCPs with Merkle-authenticated bit queries, and polynomial IOPs with polynomial-commitment evaluations. They solve the same verification problem using different encodings. PLONK develops the polynomial route for arithmetic circuits.
