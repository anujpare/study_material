IV · PLONK from circuits · Lecture §5

Fibonacci was one calculation. What about every calculation?

Fibonacci taught us how to check one kind of calculation: every step had to satisfy the same recurrence. We now know how to authenticate the polynomial and remove the verifier's challenge. What we don't yet know is how to express the correctness of a general computation as polynomial equations. Arithmetic circuits solve exactly that remaining problem.

Imagine drawing a computation as boxes connected by wires. Each box takes two numbers and either adds or multiplies them. We call a box a gate, and the whole diagram an arithmetic circuit.

The wires carry numbers from a field \(\mathbb F\), so every addition and multiplication follows that field's rules. The circuit itself is public. Some input wires carry values everybody knows; others carry private values known to the prover.

Write the public inputs as a vector \(\mathbf x\) and the private ones as \(\mathbf w\). A vector is just an ordered list of numbers. The prover wants to show there is some \(\mathbf w\) for which the circuit output is zero:

\[\boxed{C(\mathbf x,\mathbf w)=0.}\]

The corresponding language in the lecture is

\[L=\{\mathbf x\in\mathbb F^m:\exists\mathbf w\in\mathbb F^n,\ C(\mathbf x,\mathbf w)=0\}.\]

Here \(m\) and \(n\) are the numbers of public and private inputs. As before, \(\exists\) means “there exists.”

Let's calculate one circuit by hand

Take the example from the notes. The public inputs are \(x_1=2\) and \(x_2=4\), and the private input is \(w_1=3\). The circuit evaluates

\[t=(x_1\cdot x_2)\cdot(x_2+w_1).\]

We can do it with three gates. Gate 0 multiplies \(2\cdot4=8\). Gate 1 adds \(4+3=7\). Gate 2 multiplies the two results, giving \(8\cdot7=56\).

Write down what happened at every gate

We now have a circuit whose gates produce values. But our efficient ZeroTest checks polynomials, not tables of gate values. To use it, the prover must turn its entire proposed circuit execution into a polynomial that can be queried at selected points. Interpolation gives us exactly that representation.

First make the table. For each gate, record its left input, right input, and output. The lecture calls this a circuit trace.

GateLeftRightOutput
0 · \(×\)248
1 · \(+\)437
2 · \(×\)8756

There are nine entries in the gate rows. We also need to record the three original inputs \(2,4,3\). That makes twelve numbers to encode.

In general, if the circuit has \(|C|\) gates and \(|I|\) original inputs, the lecture sets

\[d=3|C|+|I|.\]

Why the factor 3? Each gate contributes exactly three entries: left, right, output. For our example, \(d=3\cdot3+3=12\).

Where do we put the twelve numbers?

Interpolation lets us choose distinct field points and make a polynomial with prescribed values at those points. The notes choose the points \(1,\omega,\omega^2,\ldots,\omega^{d-1}\), where \(\omega\) has multiplicative order \(d\).

That means \(\omega^d=1\), but none of the smaller positive powers \(\omega,\ldots,\omega^{d-1}\) equals 1. So all \(d\) points are different, exactly what interpolation needs.

\[\Omega=\{1,\omega,\omega^2,\ldots,\omega^{d-1}\}.\]
Visit all twelve points

In \(\mathbb F_{97}\), the number \(\omega=6\) has order 12. Move the exponent and see where its powers go.

The prover makes a polynomial \(f_{\mathrm{trace}}\) and assigns the values at specific powers. For each original input number \(j=1,\ldots,|I|\), set

\[f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega^{-j})=\text{input number }j.\]

For each gate \(l=0,\ldots,|C|-1\), use three consecutive powers:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega^{3l})&=\text{left input},\\ f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega^{3l+1})&=\text{right input},\\ f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega^{3l+2})&=\text{output}. \end{aligned} \]

In this multiplicative cycle, \(\omega^{-1}=\omega^{d-1}\), so the negative powers are just other points among the same \(d\) distinct powers.

Our twelve assignments, exactly as in the lecture:

PositionsValues
\(\omega^{-1},\omega^{-2},\omega^{-3}\)2, 4, 3 (original inputs)
\(\omega^{0},\omega^{1},\omega^{2}\)2, 4, 8 (gate 0)
\(\omega^{3},\omega^{4},\omega^{5}\)4, 3, 7 (gate 1)

Does such a polynomial actually exist?

Yes. We prescribed \(d=12\) values at twelve distinct points. The interpolation result gives one and only one polynomial of degree at most \(d-1=11\) that fits them. The degree bound follows from the number of points, not from a guess about the polynomial's shape.

So all the claimed gate and input values can be represented by one polynomial \(f_{\mathrm{trace}}\). We have not checked that the values are correct yet. We've only arranged them so they can be checked algebraically. \(\square\)

We can now ask \(f_{\mathrm{trace}}\) for any recorded gate value. But interpolation doesn't tell us whether the recorded values are correct: it can encode a fake trace just as easily as an honest one. Our next task is to turn circuit correctness into a few polynomial identities. First, though, notice why we chose the special powers of \(\omega\) instead of ordinary consecutive numbers. They make a large part of the verification much cheaper.

Why choose these unusual points?

In the trace encoding, we deliberately used the powers \(1,\omega,\omega^2,\ldots\) instead of ordinary positions. It may have seemed like an unnecessary complication. But recall the one operation that was still expensive in Fibonacci: calculating a vanishing polynomial as a long product. The special points let us replace that product with a single power.

For the full set \(\Omega=\{1,\omega,\ldots,\omega^{d-1}\}\), the vanishing polynomial is

\[Z_\Omega(X)=\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}(X-\omega^j).\]

That product has \(d\) factors. But look at another polynomial, \(X^d-1\). At each point \(X=\omega^j\), its value is

\[(\omega^j)^d-1=(\omega^d)^j-1=1-1=0.\]

So \(X^d-1\) has exactly the same \(d\) designated roots. In fact, the two polynomials are equal, and we can prove it without expanding the large product.

Prove \(Z_\Omega(X)=X^d-1\)

Both polynomials have degree \(d\), and both have leading coefficient 1. Subtract them:

\[h(X)=(X^d-1)-Z_\Omega(X).\]

The two \(X^d\) terms cancel. So either \(h\) is identically zero or its degree is at most \(d-1\). But at each of the \(d\) different points \(\omega^j\), both original polynomials are zero; hence \(h(\omega^j)=0\).

If \(h\) were a nonzero polynomial of degree at most \(d-1\), our root theorem would allow only \(d-1\) roots. We have \(d\) roots. That's impossible. Therefore \(h\) must be the zero polynomial.

\[\boxed{Z_\Omega(X)=X^d-1.}\]

We've replaced a product of \(d\) factors with a single power and a subtraction. That's the reason this special set of points is useful. \(\square\)

Is taking a large power expensive?

Not if we use repeated squaring. To compute \(r^{12}\), first compute \(r^2\), then \(r^4=(r^2)^2\), then \(r^8=(r^4)^2\), then \(r^{12}=r^8r^4\). That's four field multiplications instead of a product with twelve separate factors.

In general, repeated squaring takes \(O(\log d)\) multiplications to compute \(r^d\), so evaluating the full-domain vanishing polynomial is inexpensive even when \(d\) is large.

Be careful about the set. The identity \(Z_\Omega(X)=X^d-1\) holds for the complete set of distinct \(d\)-th roots of unity. A smaller arbitrary subset, such as the positions of only the public inputs, generally does not have that same formula. Those checks may require other structure or preprocessing.

So choosing the roots-of-unity domain was not decoration. It makes an important part of the polynomial check fast. We now have the trace polynomial and an efficient way to evaluate the full-domain vanishing polynomial. The remaining question is what equations the trace must satisfy to count as a real circuit execution. There are four families, and each catches a different way of cheating.

How do we know the trace is for the right problem?

The prover has encoded a table in \(f_{\mathrm{trace}}\). But any table can be interpolated into a polynomial, including a completely invented one. To turn this into a proof, we need conditions that force the table to be the execution of our circuit. Start with the two things the verifier knows for sure: the public input and the answer it expects.

Let's handle those two possibilities first. After this we'll worry about whether the gates and wires were consistent.

1. Did the final output equal zero?

Recall that gate \(l\) stores its output at position \(\omega^{3l+2}\). The last gate is numbered \(|C|-1\), so its output lives at exponent \(3(|C|-1)+2=3|C|-1\).

There is only one value to check:

\[\boxed{f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega^{3|C|-1})=0.}\]

For the three-gate picture in the notes, the final value is 56, so this zero-output check fails. That's correct: the picture calculates 56, rather than checking that the answer equals a target. When we append the final target-check gate, the correct output is zero.

2. Did the prover use our public inputs?

Suppose the verifier gave public input values \(x_1,\ldots,x_m\). The prover is supposed to put them at \(\omega^{-1},\ldots,\omega^{-m}\). We can't simply trust that it did so.

The verifier makes its own polynomial \(f_{\mathrm{in}}\) from the public values, satisfying

\[f_{\mathrm{in}}(\omega^{-j})=x_j,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m.\]

Why invent this polynomial? Because we already know how to test whether two polynomials agree at a chosen set of points. Define their difference:

\[g(X)=f_{\mathrm{trace}}(X)-f_{\mathrm{in}}(X).\]

At public input position \(a=\omega^{-j}\), the condition \(g(a)=0\) means

\[ f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega^{-j})-f_{\mathrm{in}}(\omega^{-j})=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega^{-j})=x_j. \]

So the entire public-input check is exactly the statement that \(g\) vanishes on the set

\[\Omega_I=\{\omega^{-1},\ldots,\omega^{-m}\}.\]

We've already learned to prove that with ZeroTest: the prover supplies a quotient for \(g\) over \(\Omega_I\), and the verifier checks the identity using authenticated random evaluations. It must also check that the claimed \(g\) really is \(f_{\mathrm{trace}}-f_{\mathrm{in}}\), not an unrelated polynomial.

What does a successful input check establish?

If the ZeroTest identity and the consistency equation hold, then \(g\) is zero at every public input position. At position \(\omega^{-j}\), that gives \(f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega^{-j})=f_{\mathrm{in}}(\omega^{-j})=x_j\). So every prescribed public value in the trace agrees with what the verifier knows.

The test checks exactly the public-input requirement. It does not yet check whether the gates computed correctly with those inputs. \(\square\)

Why not open all \(m\) input positions directly? That would need \(m\) polynomial opening proofs, growing linearly with the public input length. Our definition allows the verifier to read its \(m\) public values, but the proof itself is supposed to remain succinct.

The output and public-input checks tie the proposed trace to the problem we're trying to solve. But they still don't tell us how the table reached that output. A cheater could write correct endpoints and arbitrary intermediate numbers. Our next check forces every individual gate to perform its prescribed addition or multiplication.

Can one equation check both kinds of gates?

We have fixed the public inputs and final output. But a fake trace could still contain wrong calculations in the middle. If we check every gate separately, we'd lose the efficiency we wanted. So we'll describe all the addition and multiplication gates using one polynomial whose required zeros certify the local arithmetic.

Start with one gate. Call its left input \(L\), right input \(R\), and output \(O\). For an addition gate, the condition is \(L+R=O\). For a multiplication gate, it's \(LR=O\).

Introduce a number \(s\), called the selector. Set \(s=1\) for addition and \(s=0\) for multiplication. Now examine this single equation:

\[\boxed{s(L+R)+(1-s)LR-O=0.}\]

Why does it work? When \(s=1\), the multiplication term disappears and we get \(L+R-O=0\). When \(s=0\), the addition term disappears and we get \(LR-O=0\). We haven't approximated anything: it's exactly the right equation for either gate.

Put the selector into a polynomial

Every gate has a public type. Build a selector polynomial \(f_{\mathrm{sel}}\) whose value at the start of gate \(l\) is its selector:

\[ f_{\mathrm{sel}}(\omega^{3l})= \begin{cases} 0&\text{if gate }l\text{ multiplies},\\ 1&\text{if gate }l\text{ adds}. \end{cases} \]

Why store this in a polynomial? Because we can now use it together with \(f_{\mathrm{trace}}\) to form one gate-checking polynomial. At the start of a gate, the trace values at \(X,\omega X,\omega^2X\) are exactly \(L,R,O\). Replace the three letters by those evaluations:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{\mathrm{gates}}(X)={}&f_{\mathrm{sel}}(X) \bigl[f_{\mathrm{trace}}(X)+f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega X)\bigr]\\ &+\bigl(1-f_{\mathrm{sel}}(X)\bigr) f_{\mathrm{trace}}(X)f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega X)\\ &-f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega^2X). \end{aligned} \]

It looks long, but it's simply our one-gate equation with polynomial evaluations in place of \(L,R,O\).

At each gate-start position \(X=\omega^{3l}\), the equation \(f_{\mathrm{gates}}(X)=0\) is exactly equivalent to the gate's arithmetic being correct. Therefore the prover must show that \(f_{\mathrm{gates}}\) is zero on all gate-start positions:

\[ \Omega_{\mathrm{gates}}= \{1,\omega^3,\omega^6,\ldots,\omega^{3(|C|-1)}\}. \]

This is another ZeroTest. Its quotient identity can be checked at a random point after the relevant polynomials are committed to.

What does this prove—and what doesn't it prove?

For every gate \(l\), substitute \(X=\omega^{3l}\). The selector becomes the gate's fixed operation bit, and the three trace evaluations become that gate's left, right and output values. If the gate polynomial is zero there, the required addition or multiplication relation holds.

Thus a correct ZeroTest proves that every individual gate is evaluated correctly. It says nothing yet about whether a gate has actually used the output of the preceding gate connected to it. That's a different condition. \(\square\)

One efficiency detail: \(f_{\mathrm{sel}}\) is large, but it depends only on the circuit diagram, not on the witness. We can compute and commit to it once when preparing the circuit, rather than rebuilding it for every input.

There is still one loophole. Gate 2 might correctly calculate \(9\times7=63\) even though gate 0 actually output 8 and is supposed to feed gate 2. Each gate would pass its arithmetic test, but the circuit would be false. That's why we must also check that the values of connected wires agree across different gate rows.

Three correct calculations can still give a false circuit

The last lesson found a loophole in our gate test. The verifier checks every gate's arithmetic in isolation, so a dishonest prover might feed gate 2 a different number from the one gate 0 actually produced. Let's see why that makes a false trace pass the earlier tests—and how the wiring check closes the gap.

No. In our actual circuit, the output of gate 0 is wired to the left input of gate 2. Gate 0 produced 8, so gate 2 must receive 8—not 9. A calculation can be locally correct and still use a value from the wrong wire.

This is why PLONK has a separate wiring check. Wherever the same logical wire appears in the trace, the two polynomial evaluations must agree.

In our example, gate 0's output is at \(\omega^2\), and gate 2's left input is at \(\omega^6\). Gate 1's output is at \(\omega^5\), and gate 2's right input is at \(\omega^7\). So two required equalities are

\[ f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega^2)=f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega^6), \qquad f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega^5)=f_{\mathrm{trace}}(\omega^7). \]
Make the trace lie in different ways

Change the proposed trace. Watch which arithmetic equations pass and which wire connections fail. The last gate checks the public target, so a correct circuit has output zero.

How do we express all the wire equalities?

The lecture introduces a rotation polynomial \(f_{\mathrm{rot}}\). For each point \(a\) in the trace domain \(\Omega\), it identifies another position carrying the same logical wire. Repeating these mappings visits all occurrences of that wire.

So the intended relation is

\[ f_{\mathrm{trace}}(a) =f_{\mathrm{trace}}(f_{\mathrm{rot}}(a)), \qquad a\in\Omega. \]

Why would checking these relations suffice?

Take any one logical wire. Suppose its positions form a cycle \(a_1\to a_2\to\cdots\to a_k\to a_1\) under \(f_{\mathrm{rot}}\). If the displayed equation holds at every position in the cycle, then the value at \(a_1\) equals the value at \(a_2\); that equals the value at \(a_3\); and so on. By ordinary transitivity of equality, every occurrence has exactly the same value.

So these equalities are precisely what we need to prevent different occurrences of the same wire from carrying contradictory numbers. Together with the gate equations, they make the trace describe one genuine circuit computation. \(\square\)

The rotation polynomial depends on the fixed wiring diagram, so like the selector it can be prepared once for a circuit.

The lecture sketches this wiring method but does not describe the full polynomial IOP that proves these equations efficiently. We have proved what the wire equalities imply, not the security of the omitted wiring subprotocol. IV · PLONK from circuits · Lecture §6

Put all the pieces together

We started by asking how to verify an expensive computation without repeating it. We now have a trace represented by a polynomial and four checks designed to catch every type of false circuit execution. It's time to prove that those checks really do answer our original question, and then see how the proof can be short.

First, prepare what does not change

The polynomial commitment scheme runs a universal setup for a chosen maximum polynomial or circuit size, producing public parameters \(pp\). This setup does not depend on the particular circuit \(C\).

Next, for a particular circuit, calculate the selector \(f_{\mathrm{sel}}\) and rotation polynomial \(f_{\mathrm{rot}}\), then commit to them. Their contents depend only on the gates and wires of that circuit—not on the private witness. So if the circuit is used repeatedly, this circuit-specific preprocessing needs to be done only once.

Then the prover does the real computation

For public input \(\mathbf x\) and witness \(\mathbf w\), the prover evaluates the circuit, records every gate's inputs and output, and interpolates the trace polynomial \(f_{\mathrm{trace}}\). It commits to the trace and to the auxiliary polynomials and quotients needed for the input, gate, and wiring checks.

The verifier uses short polynomial openings and random points to test the claimed equations. The entire logic rests on just four requirements:

1. Output. The final gate's trace value must be zero.

2. Public input. The designated trace positions must contain the actual public values.

3. Gates. Every gate's two inputs and output must satisfy its selected addition or multiplication equation.

4. Wiring. Every occurrence of the same logical wire must carry the same value.

If all four hold, why is the statement true?

Start at the circuit's inputs. Requirement 2 fixes the public ones. The private input positions give us a candidate witness \(\mathbf w\). Requirement 4 means each wire has one consistent value wherever it is used.

Now go through the gates in computation order. For the first gate, its inputs are the specified wire values, and requirement 3 says the output is their correct sum or product. Because wiring is consistent, the next gate sees exactly the output values delivered by earlier gates. Requirement 3 then makes its output correct too. Continue through the whole circuit.

We have now shown that the recorded trace must equal a genuine evaluation of \(C(\mathbf x,\mathbf w)\). Finally, requirement 1 says its output is zero. Therefore

\[\boxed{C(\mathbf x,\mathbf w)=0.}\]

That's the missing last line of the argument. The four conditions don't merely describe a computation; together they force the existence of a witness that satisfies the public circuit statement. \(\square\)

And what makes the proof short?

Polynomial IOPs let the verifier test each family of constraints using a few evaluations rather than examining every gate. Polynomial commitments authenticate those evaluations. Finally, Fiat–Shamir derives the random challenges from the earlier commitments, so the verifier no longer needs to interact with the prover.

For suitable commitment schemes and full subprotocols, the lecture targets proof size and verification time of the form

\[ |\pi|=O(\lambda,\operatorname{polylog}|C|), \qquad T_V=O(\lambda,|\mathbf x|,\operatorname{polylog}|C|). \]

The symbol \(|C|\) measures circuit size. These are the course's target bounds, not timings obtained from the little three-gate example.

We should distinguish the result we proved from the machinery the notes don't fully develop. We proved that a trace satisfying all four conditions is a satisfying circuit execution. The end-to-end cryptographic theorem also needs secure polynomial commitments, the omitted wiring IOP, degree guarantees, and the Fiat–Shamir security argument. The notes explain the blueprint rather than proving every implementation detail.

The same construction applies to a long calculation like the repeated-function example from our first lesson: represent its steps as a circuit, commit to the corresponding trace, and prove the gate and wire relations. The prover still performs the full calculation; the savings come from not making every verifier repeat it.

The original question, answered

What was too expensive? Checking each step of a long witness.

What changed? A polynomial represents the trace; algebraic identities represent the checks; random evaluations detect false identities; polynomial commitments authenticate those evaluations; and Fiat–Shamir makes the challenges reproducible without interaction.

Why should we believe the result? The four circuit conditions force a satisfying execution, and the polynomial root bounds explain why a false identity is unlikely to pass—subject to the additional commitment, wiring-IOP, and Fiat–Shamir assumptions stated above.
