Lecture §5 · The PLONK IOP

How do we move from Fibonacci to an arbitrary computation?

The Fibonacci protocol worked because every step obeyed one recurrence. A general program may add, multiply, and reuse earlier results in different places. To use the same polynomial method, we first need to express any such computation as a collection of simple equations. The lecture uses an arithmetic circuit for this.

An arithmetic circuit \(C\) is a collection of gates connected by wires. Each gate takes two field elements and either adds or multiplies them. A wire carries a value from an input or earlier gate to a later gate. The circuit specifies which operations to perform and which values they use.

What statement will the prover prove?

Some inputs are public and known to the verifier; collect them in a vector \(\mathbf x\in\mathbb F^m\). Other inputs form the private witness \(\mathbf w\in\mathbb F^n\). A vector here is just an ordered list of field elements. The lecture defines the language

\[ L=\{\mathbf x\in\mathbb F^m:\exists\,\mathbf w\in\mathbb F^n,\ C(\mathbf x,\mathbf w)=0\}. \]

In words, the public statement \(\mathbf x\) is true if some private input \(\mathbf w\) makes the circuit output zero. The zero-output convention is convenient: to prove that a computation produces a public target \(y\), we can construct a circuit whose final result is the computed value minus \(y\), and ask whether that difference is zero.

Why does this cover the NP statements we started with? An ordinary NP verifier checks a public statement and a witness by performing elementary computational steps. Those steps can be represented by a circuit, and its acceptance condition can be expressed as a zero-output check. The circuit is therefore a structured representation of the original witness-checking problem.

In the field \(\mathbb F_2\), multiplication of bits is AND and addition is XOR, so Boolean circuits are a special case. This connects arithmetic circuits to familiar Boolean computation without changing the underlying proof goal.

We will keep the original three-gate example unchanged while learning the trace encoding.

What could a dishonest trace do?

Even if each gate performs its own operation correctly, a later gate might use a value different from the output of the gate wired to it. For example, gate 0 might output 8 while gate 2 uses 9 as its left input. So we will eventually need both gate arithmetic checks and wire consistency checks.

Lecture §5.1 · From circuits to polynomials

How can a polynomial represent the entire circuit trace?

The circuit gives us the operations to verify, but reading every gate value would defeat succinctness. We will store the proposed execution as evaluations of one polynomial, just as we stored Fibonacci values. First we must count the values and assign a distinct evaluation point to each.

Count the trace positions

Every gate contributes three entries: left input, right input, and output. If \(|C|\) is the number of gates, that gives \(3|C|\) entries. We must also store the original public and private inputs, whose total count is \(|I|\). The lecture defines

\[\boxed{d=3|C|+|I|.}\]

For the three-gate circuit, \(|C|=3\) and \(|I|=3\), so \(d=12\). We need twelve distinct evaluation points. The lecture uses the bound \(\deg f_{\rm trace}\le d\); interpolation through twelve points actually gives a polynomial of degree at most \(d-1=11\).

Why choose powers of a root of unity?

We could interpolate at any twelve distinct points. But later the verifier must evaluate a vanishing polynomial for the entire set. Choosing special points makes that calculation cheaper. Pick an element \(\omega\) of multiplicative order \(d\), meaning \(\omega^d=1\) and no smaller positive power equals 1. Its first \(d\) powers are therefore distinct:

\[\Omega=\{1,\omega,\omega^2,\ldots,\omega^{d-1}\}.\]

For a concrete twelve-point example, use \(\mathbb F_{97}\) and \(\omega=6\). It has order 12. The interactive figure shows the twelve distinct values \(6^j\bmod97\). The choice of field and root is illustrative; a real scheme chooses parameters appropriate to the circuit and security requirements.

Where does each trace entry go?

Call the interpolating polynomial \(f_{\rm trace}(X)\). The lecture assigns the original input numbered \(j\) to \(\omega^{-j}\). Negative powers are valid because \(\omega^{-j}=\omega^{d-j}\) when \(\omega^d=1\). For each gate numbered \(l=0,\ldots,|C|-1\), three consecutive powers store its left input, right input and output:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{-j})&=\text{original input }j,\\ f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{3l})&=\text{left input of gate }l,\\ f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{3l+1})&=\text{right input of gate }l,\\ f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{3l+2})&=\text{output of gate }l. \end{aligned} \]
Trace entryPolynomial positionsValues

Why does such a polynomial exist and why is it unique?

We have prescribed twelve values at twelve distinct field points. Interpolation constructs a polynomial of degree at most 11 through those values. If two degree-at-most-11 polynomials agreed at all twelve points, their difference would have twelve roots but degree at most 11, contradicting the root theorem. Therefore the trace polynomial is unique for the proposed table. \(\square\)

Uniqueness does not imply correctness. Any dishonest table also has a unique interpolating polynomial. The verifier must still check whether the stored numbers describe a genuine execution.

We chose the powers of \(\omega\) partly to make a vanishing polynomial cheap to evaluate. Before deriving the four circuit checks, let's prove that advantage: for the complete set of these powers, the vanishing polynomial is simply \(X^d-1\).

Lecture §§3 and 5.2 · Efficient vanishing polynomials

Why do the roots of unity make verification cheaper?

The Fibonacci verifier had to evaluate a product with one factor for every required recurrence position. That becomes expensive for a large computation. We chose the circuit's evaluation points as powers of a root of unity because their vanishing polynomial has a simpler form. We will prove that form rather than merely assert it.

Recall the set \(\Omega=\{1,\omega,\ldots,\omega^{d-1}\}\), where \(\omega\) has multiplicative order \(d\). The polynomial that vanishes at precisely these \(d\) points is

\[Z_\Omega(X)=\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}(X-\omega^j).\]

By the definition of \(\omega\), each point \(\omega^j\) satisfies \((\omega^j)^d=(\omega^d)^j=1\). Therefore every point of \(\Omega\) is also a root of \(X^d-1\). That observation suggests a much shorter expression for the vanishing polynomial, but we still need to show the two polynomials are equal.

Prove \(Z_\Omega(X)=X^d-1\)

Both polynomials have degree \(d\) and leading coefficient 1. Their difference therefore has degree at most \(d-1\), because the leading \(X^d\) terms cancel.

At every one of the \(d\) distinct points of \(\Omega\), both polynomials are zero. Their difference consequently has at least \(d\) roots. The root theorem says a nonzero polynomial of degree at most \(d-1\) cannot have \(d\) distinct roots. Thus the difference must be the zero polynomial, and

\[\boxed{Z_\Omega(X)=X^d-1.}\]

We have proved the identity, not just checked it on a numerical example. \(\square\)

How much work does this save?

Evaluating the original product at a point \(r\) requires roughly \(d\) factors. Evaluating \(r^d-1\) is much faster using repeated squaring. For example, \(r^{12}\) can be obtained by computing \(r^2\), \(r^4=(r^2)^2\), \(r^8=(r^4)^2\), and then \(r^{12}=r^8r^4\). The number of multiplications grows as \(O(\log d)\), rather than \(O(d)\).

This formula applies to the complete roots-of-unity domain. The public-input positions and gate-start positions are subsets of that domain. Their vanishing polynomials require their own structure or preprocessing; we must not substitute \(X^d-1\) for an arbitrary subset.

The trace polynomial is now represented on a domain whose full vanishing polynomial is inexpensive to evaluate. But interpolation does not certify the trace. We must still prove that the stored values are the right public inputs, that each gate performs the correct operation, that wire copies agree, and that the output satisfies the claim. The next lessons derive these four checks.

Lecture §5.2 · Conditions 1 and 2

Does the trace describe the public statement we are proving?

The prover can interpolate any table of numbers. We need four constraints that make the table a valid circuit execution. The first two connect it to the particular public statement: the final output must be correct, and the public input entries must equal the values the verifier supplied.

1. Check the output

For gate \(l\), the trace stores its output at \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{3l+2})\). The last gate has index \(|C|-1\), so its output position is \(3(|C|-1)+2=3|C|-1\). The formal zero-output statement therefore requires

\[\boxed{f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{3|C|-1})=0.}\]

In the lecture's pictured three-gate example, the output is 56, so the corresponding illustrative target check is \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega^8)=56\). That is not the formal zero-output condition. A circuit with an added target-comparison operation would have a different trace and final output position.

2. Check the public inputs

The verifier knows the public input values \(x_1,\ldots,x_m\). The trace is supposed to store them at \(\omega^{-1},\ldots,\omega^{-m}\). We cannot simply trust the prover's claim that those entries are correct.

From the public values, construct an interpolation polynomial \(f_{\rm in}(X)\) satisfying \(f_{\rm in}(\omega^{-j})=x_j\) for \(j=1,\ldots,m\). This polynomial depends only on the public statement, so the verifier can calculate it without knowing the witness.

Let \(\Omega_I=\{\omega^{-1},\ldots,\omega^{-m}\}\). Define the difference polynomial

\[g(X)=f_{\rm trace}(X)-f_{\rm in}(X).\]

At position \(\omega^{-j}\), the equality \(g(\omega^{-j})=0\) is exactly the requirement \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{-j})=x_j\). Therefore all public inputs are correct if and only if \(g\) vanishes on \(\Omega_I\).

Why does this become two checks, not one?

We want to use ZeroTest on \(g\), but the prover could put an unrelated polynomial in the oracle and claim it is \(g\). So the verifier must first check the consistency identity \(g=f_{\rm trace}-f_{\rm in}\), using authenticated evaluations at a random point. Only then does ZeroTest on \(g\) establish that the trace contains the public inputs.

Why the two checks together are sufficient

If the consistency identity holds, then \(g(a)=f_{\rm trace}(a)-f_{\rm in}(a)\) for every field point \(a\). If ZeroTest establishes that \(g(a)=0\) for every \(a\in\Omega_I\), then at each public-input position \(f_{\rm trace}(a)=f_{\rm in}(a)=x_j\). Conversely, if the trace has the correct public inputs, its genuine difference polynomial vanishes on \(\Omega_I\). These are precisely the two directions we need. \(\square\)

Why not simply open every public-input position? Because that would require a separate proof for each entry, making the proof size grow linearly with the public input. The verifier's running time may depend linearly on the public statement, but the SNARG proof length must remain succinct. The input polynomial and ZeroTest let the verifier avoid a linear number of authenticated openings.

We now know that the trace starts with the right public inputs and claims the required output. But it could still contain incorrect intermediate calculations. The next constraint checks the arithmetic of every gate without opening every gate entry separately.

Lecture §5.2 · Verifying the circuit trace, condition 3

How can one equation check both addition and multiplication?

The trace may contain the correct public inputs and final output while lying about intermediate calculations. We must check every addition and multiplication gate without inspecting them individually. The selector polynomial lets us express all those gate equations as one polynomial condition.

Start with just one gate. Call its left input \(L\), its right input \(R\), and its output \(O\). For addition we need \(L+R=O\). For multiplication we need \(LR=O\).

The circuit already tells us which operation each gate performs. Encode that public choice with a bit \(s\), called a selector: set \(s=1\) for addition and \(s=0\) for multiplication. Now consider

\[\boxed{s(L+R)+(1-s)LR-O=0.}\]

Substitute \(s=1\): the multiplication part disappears, leaving \(L+R-O=0\). Substitute \(s=0\): the addition part disappears, leaving \(LR-O=0\). So the same equation checks either kind of gate exactly.

Look at the lecture's example. Gate 0 multiplies \(2\cdot4=8\), so \(s=0\) and the equation becomes \(2\cdot4-8=0\). Gate 1 adds \(4+3=7\), so \(s=1\) and it becomes \(4+3-7=0\). The formula doesn't merely resemble the right rules; it gives them exactly.

Use the trace polynomial instead of three separate numbers

Recall our encoding: at gate-start point \(X=\omega^{3l}\), the left input is \(f_{\rm trace}(X)\), the right input is \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega X)\), and the output is \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega^2X)\). The public circuit also determines the selector for each gate.

Interpolate those selector bits into a polynomial \(f_{\rm sel}(X)\) with \(f_{\rm sel}(\omega^{3l})=0\) for multiplication and 1 for addition. For the three-gate example, its values at gate starts \(1,\omega^3,\omega^6\) are 0, 1, 0.

Now replace \(L,R,O,s\) in the single-gate equation with these polynomial evaluations:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{\rm gates}(X)={}&f_{\rm sel}(X)\bigl(f_{\rm trace}(X)+f_{\rm trace}(\omega X)\bigr)\\ &+\bigl(1-f_{\rm sel}(X)\bigr)f_{\rm trace}(X)f_{\rm trace}(\omega X)\\ &-f_{\rm trace}(\omega^2X). \end{aligned} \]

Why is this useful? At the start of any gate \(l\), the equation \(f_{\rm gates}(\omega^{3l})=0\) is exactly the correct arithmetic rule for that gate. Hence every gate is evaluated correctly if and only if the gate polynomial vanishes on

\[\Omega_{\rm gates}=\{\omega^0,\omega^3,\omega^6,\ldots,\omega^{3(|C|-1)}\}.\]

That's the same pattern we proved in Fibonacci: many required zero values, one vanishing-polynomial quotient, and a random ZeroTest to check the identity without visiting every position.

What does this guarantee?

If the gate polynomial is zero at each gate-start point, substituting those points recovers the correct addition or multiplication equation for every gate, because the selector comes from the public circuit. Conversely, if every gate is evaluated correctly, those same equations are zero at every gate-start point.

We have reduced all the local gate checks to a polynomial vanishing condition. But we have not yet proved that one gate used the output of the gate actually wired to it. \(\square\)

The selector polynomial is large—its degree grows with circuit size. The verifier should not calculate it from scratch for every proof. But the selector depends only on the circuit, not on the input or private witness, so it can be computed and committed to in a one-time circuit-specific preprocessing step.

Here's the remaining loophole. Gate 2 could correctly compute \(9\cdot7=63\), even though gate 0 produced 8 and was supposed to feed 8 into gate 2. Every local gate equation might pass while the overall circuit is false. The last condition checks the wires linking the gate rows.

Lecture §5.2 · Verifying the circuit trace, condition 4

Three correct gates can still describe the wrong computation

Correct gate arithmetic is not enough. A gate could use a value different from the output of the gate connected to it. We must also check that every occurrence of a wire carries the same value. The rotation polynomial describes which trace positions must agree.

In our original circuit, gate 0 produces 8, and gate 2 must use that 8 as its left input. Gate 1 produces 7, and gate 2 must use 7 as its right input. In terms of the trace polynomial, the required equalities are

\[ f_{\rm trace}(\omega^2)=f_{\rm trace}(\omega^6), \qquad f_{\rm trace}(\omega^5)=f_{\rm trace}(\omega^7). \]

And we must also ensure the original input entries agree with the copies inside the gates. For example, public \(x_2=4\) appears at \(\omega^{-2}\), gate 0's right input at \(\omega^1\), and gate 1's left input at \(\omega^3\). All three places refer to one logical wire and must contain the same number.

Why each of the four checks is necessary

Choose a proposed trace. Each dishonest case satisfies three checks and violates exactly one; the failing condition is therefore necessary. This uses the original three-gate circuit. Its output is compared with the public target 56; the formal zero-output statement would require an additional target-check gate and a new trace.

How can we describe all wire equalities at once?

Take any logical wire and list every trace position where its value appears. Put those positions in a cycle. For the input \(x_2\), for instance, we can make a cycle through \(\omega^{-2}\), \(\omega^1\), \(\omega^3\), and back to \(\omega^{-2}\).

The lecture encodes the mapping from each position to the next position carrying the same wire using a rotation polynomial \(f_{\rm rot}\). It is a polynomial representation of a permutation of the trace positions, determined entirely by the circuit wiring. It is not merely the operation \(X\mapsto\omega X\).

The intended consistency condition is

\[\boxed{f_{\rm trace}(a)=f_{\rm trace}(f_{\rm rot}(a))\quad\text{for all }a\in\Omega.}\]

Why do these equalities enforce the actual wires?

Suppose one logical wire appears at positions \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\), ordered as a cycle \(a_1\to a_2\to\cdots\to a_k\to a_1\). The displayed equation forces the value at \(a_1\) to equal the value at \(a_2\); that equals the value at \(a_3\), and so on. By ordinary transitivity of equality, every occurrence carries the same number.

Conversely, if each logical wire really does carry one consistent value at every occurrence, then all these equalities hold. So checking these relations is equivalent to checking correct wire copying.

This closes the loophole left by local gate equations. Once wire copies are consistent, the gate calculations refer to the actual connected circuit, not independent rows of arithmetic. \(\square\)

Like the selector polynomial, \(f_{\rm rot}\) depends only on the public circuit, not on its witness. It can therefore be computed and committed to in preprocessing. The lecture explicitly does not give the detailed polynomial IOP for efficiently checking these rotation relations; we should understand the logical condition and its purpose without inventing a full security proof that isn't in the source.

Lecture §6 · A PLONK SNARG

Why do these checks prove the circuit statement?

We have four requirements: correct public inputs, correct output, correct gate arithmetic, and consistent wires. We must now prove that satisfying all four means the prover has supplied a genuine execution of the public circuit. Then we can assemble the polynomial checks into the final SNARG construction.

In the previous experiment, we constructed four dishonest traces. Each passed three constraint families but failed the fourth. This shows that none of the four checks can simply be omitted. We must now establish the other direction: that passing all four really is sufficient.

From a valid trace to a satisfying witness

Take a trace that passes all four checks. The public-input check fixes the known inputs to the values the verifier supplied. The other original input positions determine a candidate private witness \(\mathbf w\).

Now process the circuit in gate order. For the first gate, its input wires have definite values. The gate equation forces its output to be the correct sum or product. The wiring check forces every later occurrence of that output wire to carry exactly that same value. So the next gate receives the outputs specified by the circuit, rather than values invented independently by the prover.

Repeat this reasoning through all gates. By induction on the gate order, the whole trace is a genuine evaluation of \(C(\mathbf x,\mathbf w)\). Finally, the output check says that this genuine evaluation reaches zero. Therefore

\[\boxed{C(\mathbf x,\mathbf w)=0.}\]

We have proved the logical implication the protocol needs: if the four constraint families hold, the trace supplies an actual witness to the public circuit statement. This is why checking them is sufficient. \(\square\)

The proof also explains soundness. If the public statement is false, every proposed trace must violate at least one of the four conditions. Otherwise that trace would provide a satisfying witness, contradicting the false statement. The polynomial tests must detect whichever condition fails. Their error bounds require fixed, degree-bounded polynomials, and the lecture leaves the detailed wiring test unspecified.

How is the final system organized?

The lecture divides the work into three stages, because some calculations depend only on the circuit, while others depend on the witness for a particular proof.

Universal setup. Run the polynomial commitment scheme's setup to obtain public parameters \(pp\). This setup supports some maximum degree or circuit size but does not depend on a particular circuit \(C\).

Circuit-specific preprocessing. Given \(C\), build its selector polynomial \(f_{\rm sel}\) and rotation polynomial \(f_{\rm rot}\), commit to them, and publish the commitments as part of the circuit's verification information. This step takes work proportional to circuit size \(O(|C|)\) but can be reused for many different inputs to the same circuit.

Proof and verification. For public input \(\mathbf x\) and witness \(\mathbf w\), compute the circuit trace, interpolate and commit to \(f_{\rm trace}\), and produce the polynomial checks summarized below.

The four checks in the lecture's final protocol

1. Output: authenticate the trace polynomial's value at the final gate's output position and check it is zero.

2. Public inputs: construct \(f_{\rm in}\) from the public values, check that the claimed difference \(f_{\rm trace}-f_{\rm in}\) is consistent with the trace, and use ZeroTest over the public-input positions with a quotient polynomial.

3. Gate arithmetic: use the preprocessed selector polynomial and the trace polynomial to form \(f_{\rm gates}\). The prover supplies its vanishing-polynomial quotient over all gate-start positions. The verifier checks the resulting identity using authenticated random evaluations.

4. Wiring: use the preprocessed rotation polynomial to establish that all occurrences of the same logical wire have equal values. The lecture sketches this additional polynomial IOP rather than deriving it completely.

Every random evaluation point that the verifier would choose interactively can then be derived using Fiat–Shamir in the random-oracle model, producing a non-interactive proof string. The lecture also points out that the choice of polynomial commitment scheme matters: some schemes need a trusted setup while others do not; some are designed for post-quantum security while others are not. These are choices of the cryptographic implementation, not changes to the four logical circuit constraints.

Why is it supposed to be succinct?

The prover still performs the full circuit computation and prepares the polynomial commitments and quotients. But the verifier does not inspect every gate. It authenticates a small number of polynomial evaluations and checks a small number of identities.

Let \(|C|\) mean the number of circuit gates, \(|\mathbf x|\) the size of the public input, \(|\pi|\) the proof length, and \(T_V\) the verification time. The lecture's intended bounds are

\[ |\pi|=O\bigl(\lambda,\operatorname{polylog}|C|\bigr), \qquad T_V=O\bigl(\lambda,|\mathbf x|,\operatorname{polylog}|C|\bigr). \]

These are target asymptotic bounds for suitable implementations, not costs derived from our tiny example. Their significance is that the verifier may read the public statement, but it should not redo work proportional to the full circuit.

We should be precise about the scope of the notes. We've explained why the four conditions imply a satisfying circuit execution, the polynomial tools for checking the stated identities, and the commitment/Fiat–Shamir blueprint. The lecture does not fully prove a concrete polynomial commitment construction or its entire wiring IOP, so a complete end-to-end SNARG security theorem needs those additional components.

Nor has the lecture shown that the proof hides the witness. Zero knowledge is a further property that may be added by randomization; it isn't part of the SNARG definition used here.

Return to the original million-step computation. The prover still does the expensive work, but it can encode the execution as a circuit trace, and the verifier can check authenticated polynomial identities rather than recomputing every step. The four constraints are what tie those short checks back to the actual computation. That's the full reason the construction is useful—not just a collection of unrelated polynomial tricks.
