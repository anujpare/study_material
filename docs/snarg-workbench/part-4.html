IV · PLONK from circuits · Lecture §5

Why PLONK starts with an arithmetic circuit

Question. Fibonacci involved one special recurrence. How do we express a completely different computation in a form that the same polynomial-testing technique can verify?

An arithmetic circuit consists of wires carrying elements of a field \(\mathbb F\) and gates that add or multiply their two input values. The circuit structure is public. The values carried by internal wires depend on its inputs.

Separate the inputs into a public vector \(\mathbf x\in\mathbb F^m\) (the statement) and a private vector \(\mathbf w\in\mathbb F^n\) (the witness). The verifier wants to know whether a witness makes the designated circuit's output equal to zero:

\[L=\{\mathbf x\in\mathbb F^m:\exists\,\mathbf w\in\mathbb F^n,\ C(\mathbf x,\mathbf w)=0\}.\]

The symbolic vectors are written in bold, while individual field values and functions are not. A true statement is one for which at least one witness makes all gate computations consistent and the final output zero.

The lecture's three-gate example

Take public \(x_1=2,x_2=4\) and private \(w_1=3\). The circuit computes \(t=(x_1x_2)(x_2+w_1)\): gate 0 multiplies \(2\cdot4=8\), gate 1 adds \(4+3=7\), and gate 2 multiplies \(8\cdot7=56\).

This diagram is a computation, not yet a zero-output statement: its final output is \(56\). To prove that this computation equals a public target \(56\), introduce the constraint \(C(\mathbf x,w_1)=t-56=0\).

Within arithmetic gates over \(\mathbb F_{97}\), the negative of \(56\) is \(41\), because \(56+41=97\equiv0\pmod{97}\). Therefore an additional final addition gate with inputs \(t,41\) and output \(0\) expresses the equality to the target. We keep the original three-gate trace when studying the lecture's exact indexing and use the four-gate augmented circuit when testing the complete zero-output statement.

What the circuit representation provides. Any valid computation trace must satisfy a finite collection of simple equations: one equation per gate, equality at copied wires, agreement with public inputs, and a zero final output. The remaining construction turns all of these constraints into polynomial checks.

IV · PLONK from circuits · Lecture §5.1

One polynomial for an entire circuit trace

Question. Where will each input and intermediate wire value be stored so that the verifier can later address it through a polynomial evaluation?

The circuit trace is the table of values for every gate's left input, right input, and output. We also reserve positions for the original circuit inputs. If there are \(|C|\) gates and \(|I|\) original inputs, the lecture sets

\[d=3|C|+|I|.\]

For the three-gate example, \(|C|=3\), \(|I|=3\), and \(d=12\). We need 12 distinct field points. Rather than choosing arbitrary points, the notes choose the powers of a field element \(\omega\) of exact multiplicative order \(d\):

\[\Omega=\{1,\omega,\omega^2,\ldots,\omega^{d-1}\},\qquad\omega^d=1.\]

The word “exact” matters. An element could satisfy \(\omega^{12}=1\) while having order 3, in which case its first twelve powers would repeat and could not encode twelve independent values.

A real 12th root of unity

We work in \(\mathbb F_{97}\), where \(\omega=6\) has multiplicative order exactly 12. The points are the values \(6^j\bmod97\).

Interpolate a polynomial \(f_{\rm trace}\) through the following prescribed evaluations:

\[\begin{aligned} f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{-j})&=\text{original input }j \quad (j=1,\ldots,|I|),\\ f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{3l})&=\text{left input of gate }l,\\ f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{3l+1})&=\text{right input of gate }l,\\ f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{3l+2})&=\text{output of gate }l, \end{aligned}\]

where \(l=0,\ldots,|C|-1\). Negative exponents refer to the same multiplicative cycle: \(\omega^{-1}=\omega^{d-1}\), \(\omega^{-2}=\omega^{d-2}\), and so forth.

Does a suitable trace polynomial exist?

We have prescribed exactly \(d=12\) values at \(12\) distinct field points. The interpolation fact proved earlier guarantees exactly one polynomial of degree at most \(d-1=11\) taking those values. This explains the example's degree-11 polynomial. The lecture sometimes writes a looser upper bound \(d\); using degree at most \(d-1\) is sufficient here.

The prover can therefore encode its entire trace as evaluations of one degree-bounded polynomial. We have only represented the purported computation; verification of its correctness has not happened yet. \(\square\)

IV · PLONK from circuits · Lecture §§3 and 5.2

Why choose roots of unity?

Question. Our Fibonacci ZeroTest still required a large product \(Z_\Omega(r)\). How does the choice of polynomial evaluation points make that operation inexpensive?

For the complete domain \(\Omega=\{1,\omega,\ldots,\omega^{d-1}\}\) of distinct \(d\)-th roots of unity, define the vanishing polynomial

\[Z_\Omega(X)=\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}(X-\omega^j).\]

Each \(\omega^j\) is a root of \(X^d-1\), because \((\omega^j)^d=(\omega^d)^j=1\). Both \(Z_\Omega\) and \(X^d-1\) are monic polynomials of degree \(d\).

Prove \(Z_\Omega(X)=X^d-1\)

Consider \(h(X)=(X^d-1)-Z_\Omega(X)\). Both terms have leading coefficient 1 at degree \(d\), so that leading term cancels and \(\deg h\le d-1\), unless \(h\) is identically zero.

For every \(a=\omega^j\in\Omega\), both terms evaluate to zero. Thus \(h\) has all \(d\) distinct points of \(\Omega\) as roots. If \(h\ne0\), the root theorem would allow at most \(d-1\) roots, a contradiction. Therefore \(h=0\).

\[\boxed{Z_\Omega(X)=X^d-1.}\]

We established the identity, so the verifier can evaluate the complete-domain vanishing polynomial by computing one power \(r^d\) and subtracting one, rather than multiplying \(d\) separate factors. \(\square\)

Why the cost becomes logarithmic

Repeated squaring computes \(r^d\) with \(O(\log d)\) field multiplications. For example, to obtain \(r^{13}\), calculate \(r^2=(r)^2\), \(r^4=(r^2)^2\), \(r^8=(r^4)^2\), then multiply \(r^8r^4r\). The exponent has only four binary digits, so the number of operations depends on its bit length, not its numerical magnitude.

There is one important boundary to the claim: an arbitrary subset \(\Omega_I\subseteq\Omega\) does not generally have vanishing polynomial \(X^{|\Omega_I|}-1\). Handling such subsets can require additional structure, preprocessing or work proportional to the public input size. The lecture's fast identity here is for the full roots-of-unity domain.

What was solved. We identified the precise expensive operation left over from Fibonacci—evaluating a large vanishing-product—and replaced it for the full domain with \(r^d-1\), whose exponent can be computed in logarithmically many field multiplications.

Source: official notes, discussion after Fibonacci and §5.2, pp. 7 and 10.

IV · PLONK from circuits · Lecture §5.2, checks 1–2

Checking the output and public inputs

Question. Given a committed \(f_{\rm trace}\), how can the verifier make sure it represents the claimed public computation, rather than a conveniently different one?

1. The final output must equal zero

By the position assignment, the last gate has index \(|C|-1\), and its output is at exponent \(3(|C|-1)+2=3|C|-1\). Therefore the verifier checks the particular evaluation

\[\boxed{f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{3|C|-1})=0.}\]

This one-point query checks the output condition but not whether the prior gates were evaluated honestly. For the unaugmented three-gate example, the final value is 56, so the zero-output check would correctly reject it. The augmented target-checking circuit has a fourth gate whose output is zero.

2. The trace must contain the right public inputs

The verifier knows \(m\) public values \(x_1,\ldots,x_m\). They should appear at positions \(\Omega_I=\{\omega^{-1},\ldots,\omega^{-m}\}\). Construct a public input polynomial \(f_{\rm in}\) satisfying \(f_{\rm in}(\omega^{-j})=x_j\) for each public input. Interpolation yields degree at most \(m-1\), provided these points are distinct.

Now form the discrepancy \(g(X)=f_{\rm trace}(X)-f_{\rm in}(X)\). The encoded inputs are correct precisely when \(g\) vanishes on \(\Omega_I\):

\[\boxed{g(a)=0\quad\forall a\in\Omega_I.}\]

By Theorem 2, this is equivalent to the existence of a quotient polynomial \(q_I\) with \(g(X)=q_I(X)Z_{\Omega_I}(X)\). The prover commits to the necessary polynomials and their quotient. The verifier uses their authenticated openings at a random challenge to check the identity, while also ensuring that the claimed \(g\) corresponds to \(f_{\rm trace}-f_{\rm in}\).

Why this particular test suffices

At every public input position \(a=\omega^{-j}\), the equation \(g(a)=0\) expands to \(f_{\rm trace}(a)-f_{\rm in}(a)=0\). The verifier constructed \(f_{\rm in}(a)=x_j\), so it follows that \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega^{-j})=x_j\), which is exactly the required public-input condition.

Conversely, if all required trace inputs agree with \(x_j\), every such difference is zero, so the vanishing condition and its quotient identity hold.

Thus the ZeroTest is equivalent to agreement with all prescribed public inputs; it is not merely correlated with that agreement. Polynomial binding and consistency of the auxiliary polynomial are needed to make the random evaluations meaningful. \(\square\)

Why not simply open each input position? Doing so would require \(\Theta(m)\) polynomial openings and make the proof size depend linearly on the number of public inputs. The lecture allows verifier work proportional to \(|\mathbf x|\), but its succinctness definition does not allow a proof whose size grows linearly with \(|\mathbf x|\).

Source: official notes, §5.2 items (1)–(2) and footnote 3, pp. 9–10.

IV · PLONK from circuits · Lecture §5.2, check 3

One polynomial equation for every gate

Question. Every gate is either addition or multiplication. How can we represent both possibilities by one equation, instead of making the verifier check every gate separately?

For gate \(l\), let \(L_l,R_l,O_l\) denote its two input values and output. A correct addition gate satisfies \(L_l+R_l-O_l=0\); a correct multiplication gate satisfies \(L_lR_l-O_l=0\).

The selector \(s_l\) indicates the operation: \(s_l=1\) for addition and \(s_l=0\) for multiplication. Then both relations are represented by

\[\boxed{s_l(L_l+R_l)+(1-s_l)L_lR_l-O_l=0.}\]

To justify this transformation rather than merely assert it, substitute each possible selector value. When \(s_l=1\), the multiplication term has coefficient zero and the equation is \(L_l+R_l-O_l=0\). When \(s_l=0\), the addition term vanishes and the equation is \(L_lR_l-O_l=0\). The circuit fixes the selector at one of these two values for each gate.

Interpolate a selector polynomial \(f_{\rm sel}\) satisfying \(f_{\rm sel}(\omega^{3l})=s_l\). Because gate types are properties of the public circuit, \(f_{\rm sel}\) can be computed and committed to once in a circuit-specific preprocessing step.

Replace gate values by the trace polynomial

At position \(X=\omega^{3l}\), the values \(L_l,R_l,O_l\) are exactly \(f_{\rm trace}(X)\), \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega X)\), and \(f_{\rm trace}(\omega^2X)\). The lecture therefore defines

\[\begin{aligned} f_{\rm gates}(X)={}& f_{\rm sel}(X)\big[f_{\rm trace}(X)+f_{\rm trace}(\omega X)\big]\\ &+(1-f_{\rm sel}(X))f_{\rm trace}(X)f_{\rm trace}(\omega X)\\ &-f_{\rm trace}(\omega^2X). \end{aligned}\]

Substituting a gate position into this expression gives exactly the previously derived gate equation, not a separate approximation. Thus every gate is correct if and only if \(f_{\rm gates}(X)=0\) at every point in the gate domain \(\Omega_{\rm gates}=\{1,\omega^3,\omega^6,\ldots,\omega^{3(|C|-1)}\}\).

Theorem 2 turns that requirement into divisibility by \(Z_{\Omega_{\rm gates}}\), and ZeroTest checks the resulting identity at a random point. Because the selector and trace polynomials have degree \(O(|C|)\), \(f_{\rm gates}\) has degree \(O(|C|)\) as well: products increase degree by addition, not multiplication of the degree bounds.

What this check proves

Assume the selector evaluation at every gate position is correct, the auxiliary committed polynomial evaluations are consistent, and the ZeroTest identity holds. It follows that \(f_{\rm gates}(\omega^{3l})=0\) for every gate \(l\). Substituting the meaning of \(f_{\rm sel}\) and the three trace positions reduces this to \(L_l+R_l=O_l\) for an addition gate or \(L_lR_l=O_l\) for a multiplication gate.

Consequently all gate computations are correct as local triples. This is exactly the claimed scope of the test; it does not yet show that a gate uses the value produced by a different gate on a connected wire. \(\square\)

The remaining gap. Locally correct triples can still be connected incorrectly. The wiring check is indispensable because the same logical wire appears at multiple polynomial locations.

Source: official notes, §5.2 item (3), p. 10.

IV · PLONK from circuits · Lecture §5.2, check 4

Correct gates can form an incorrect circuit

Question. Why does proving every gate equation hold not guarantee that the values form a valid execution of the whole circuit?

The output of one gate can feed another. An honest trace must represent the same logical wire by the same field value wherever that wire appears. But the polynomial stores each occurrence at its own evaluation point, so equalities between occurrences are a separate family of constraints.

In the lecture example, the output of gate 0 must equal the left input of gate 2, and the output of gate 1 must equal the right input of gate 2:

\[\begin{aligned} f_{\rm trace}(\omega^2)&=f_{\rm trace}(\omega^6),\\ f_{\rm trace}(\omega^5)&=f_{\rm trace}(\omega^7). \end{aligned}\]

Similarly, the original input \(x_2=4\) occurs as an input and in two gate rows, so the three locations \(\omega^{-2},\omega^1,\omega^3\) must all encode 4. Local gate checks would not enforce those equalities.

Change the trace and identify the violated constraint

All arithmetic is modulo 97. Compare local gate residuals with public-input, wire-copy and final-output checks. “Broken wiring” keeps every gate locally correct to isolate the logical gap.

The rotation-polynomial abstraction

The lecture introduces a polynomial \(f_{\rm rot}\) that maps each trace position to another occurrence of the same logical wire. For example, the three copies of \(x_2\) can form a cycle \(\omega^{-2}\to\omega^1\to\omega^3\to\omega^{-2}\). If the trace value at every position equals the value at its successor, every occurrence in the cycle has the same value.

\[f_{\rm trace}(a)=f_{\rm trace}(f_{\rm rot}(a))\qquad(a\in\Omega).\]

Because the wiring is determined by the circuit rather than the witness, \(f_{\rm rot}\) can also be prepared and committed to in circuit-specific preprocessing.

Why checking every copy relation is sufficient

Take any logical wire and follow the cycle of its occurrences under \(f_{\rm rot}\). If the displayed equality holds at every position in that cycle, then the first value equals the second, the second equals the third, and so on until every occurrence has been visited. Transitivity of equality therefore forces one common value across the entire wire.

Thus the copy constraints guarantee consistent reuse of wire values. Together with correct local gate equations, they make the table describe an actual circuit evaluation rather than a collection of unrelated correct arithmetic triples. \(\square\)

Scope of the notes. The official lecture explicitly omits the full polynomial IOP for checking wiring and sketches its reduction to a ZeroTest-like condition. The equality above expresses the intended property, but it is not a complete implementation or security proof of the omitted wiring subprotocol. Simply composing polynomials and sampling once would require carefully justified degree and efficiency bounds.

Source: official notes, §5.2 item (4), pp. 10–11.

IV · PLONK from circuits · Lecture §6

Putting the PLONK SNARG together

Question. We have four circuit correctness conditions, ideal polynomial tests, and commitments. Which work happens once, and which work must be repeated for every proof?

Stage 1 · Universal setup

Run the polynomial commitment scheme's setup with a degree and circuit-size bound, producing public parameters \(pp\). “Universal” means these parameters are not tied to one particular circuit, within the allowed size bound.

Stage 2 · Circuit-specific preprocessing

From the public circuit \(C\), compute \(f_{\rm sel}\), encoding each gate type, and \(f_{\rm rot}\), encoding wire-copy structure. Commit to them and publish their commitments as circuit-specific verification information. This costs \(O(|C|)\) once, before proving many different instances of the same circuit.

Stage 3 · The prover constructs the proof

Execute \(C(\mathbf x,\mathbf w)\). Interpolate and commit to \(f_{\rm trace}\). Construct the auxiliary input-difference, gate-constraint and wiring-related polynomials and the quotients that witness their vanishing properties. Provide the short opening proofs requested by the polynomial IOP. The prover must perform the witness-dependent work; succinctness is chiefly about proof size and verification cost.

Stage 4 · The verifier checks

Verify the output condition, agreement with public input, all gate equations, and wiring consistency by authenticated polynomial evaluations and the corresponding polynomial IOP checks. A false circuit assertion must violate at least one of these constraints: otherwise the trace would be a consistent evaluation of the given circuit with the correct public input and final output zero.

Stage 5 · Remove interaction

Derive the verifier challenges using Fiat–Shamir from the statement and prior commitments. The proof becomes one transferable string.

The soundness argument: what is established and what is assumed

If every constraint holds exactly, then the public inputs are correct; the copy constraints connect each logical wire consistently; every gate computes its designated operation; and the final output is zero. Starting from the input wires and following the circuit's acyclic computation order, those properties imply the trace is an honest evaluation with a satisfying witness. Therefore a false statement cannot satisfy all constraints simultaneously.

The actual cryptographic verifier checks those identities probabilistically rather than everywhere. The proven root bounds control false acceptance of low-degree nonzero identities. Polynomial commitments must enforce consistent evaluations and declared degree bounds; Fiat–Shamir requires its random-oracle security justification; and the wiring IOP omitted by the notes needs its own soundness guarantee.

The logical circuit reduction is complete: a trace satisfying all four constraint families is a valid satisfying execution. A full end-to-end PLONK security theorem additionally depends on the cryptographic and wiring-IOP properties named above, whose constructions are not supplied in the lecture. \(\square\)

\[|\pi|=O(\lambda,\operatorname{polylog}|C|),\qquad T_V=O(\lambda,|\mathbf x|,\operatorname{polylog}|C|).\]

These are the desired succinctness bounds stated by the lecture for appropriate constructions. They are not measurements produced by the simple illustrative circuit. A choice of polynomial commitment determines concrete proof sizes and setup assumptions; the lecture also explicitly leaves zero-knowledge randomization outside this construction.

Source: official lecture, §6 (p. 11); handwritten notes (pp. 15–17) stop before the detailed PLONK circuit checks.
