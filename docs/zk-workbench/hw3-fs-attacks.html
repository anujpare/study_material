Homework: can a cheater guess the Schnorr challenge?

A cheater who doesn't know the Schnorr exponent can still make a transcript that would pass for one challenge of its choice. Fiat–Shamir makes the actual challenge come from a hash. How likely is the cheater to get lucky?

(c) Prove the bound for a prescribed guessing strategy

The cheater fixes \(u,z\), then queries \(c=H(h,u)\). Verification demands \(g^z=u h^c\). Assume \(h\ne1_G\), where \(1_G\) is the group identity, and \(G\) has prime order \(q\).

Exactly one challenge can be accepted

Rearrange the equation: \(h^c=g^zu^{-1}\). Once \(u,z\) are fixed, the right side is one fixed group element. Because \(h\ne1_G\) in a prime-order group, its \(q\) powers are all different. Exactly one of the \(q\) challenge values can satisfy the equation. A fresh uniform hash therefore succeeds with probability \(1/q\).

Now suppose the cheater tries \(Q\) different candidate inputs. Let \(E_i\) mean its \(i\)-th attempt succeeds. Each fresh random-oracle answer has conditional success probability at most \(1/q\). The union bound gives \(\Pr[E_1\lor\cdots\lor E_Q]\le Q/q\). We count all attempted candidates.

\[\Pr[\text{at least one successful guess}]\leq\min\{1,Q/q\}\]

Repeating the same hash input does not make another attempt: a random oracle returns the same stored answer each time.

Derive the attack, not just the bound

Every candidate has one winning hash value

Try a small group with \(q=11\) and \(h=8\). The cheater can pick a response \(z_i\), choose the challenge it hopes to get, say \(c_\star=2\), and set \(u_i=g^{z_i}h^{-2}\) without knowing the secret. Then it queries \(H(h,u_i)\) and hopes the output is 2.

This proves the bound for the specified guessing strategy, not for every possible attack. If the cheater makes \(Q\) oracle queries and then submits one new unqueried candidate, that extra guess must also be counted: the simple bound becomes \((Q+1)/q\).

We need \(h\ne1_G\). If \(h=1_G\), all powers \(h^c\) are identical, so the “one successful challenge” argument fails. In that case \(x=0\) is already a public witness.

(d) Why a non-interactive proof can convince a third party

NIZK proof string

A non-interactive proof \(\pi=(u,z)\) can be copied and passed to another verifier, who recomputes \(H(h,u)\) and checks the same equation. It doesn't need to talk to the prover.

Recorded interactive transcript

A recording of an interactive zero-knowledge conversation is different. The verifier could have generated an indistinguishable-looking transcript with a simulator, without a real prover participating. So a recording alone doesn't establish to a third party that the prover took part.

This says nothing about someone relaying a real conversation live. Zero knowledge does not automatically stop such attacks.

Our guessing bound is only good if the challenge space is large. With just one challenge bit, there are only two possibilities; the chance of guessing correctly is too high. That's the next exercise.
