Part II · How to keep a secret fixed

A commitment hides information without allowing it to change

A verifier needs to ask an unpredictable question, but how can it know that the prover did not alter its earlier answer after seeing the question? A commitment scheme supplies exactly the two properties we need.

Make a value fixed now; reveal it later

The prover chooses a bit \(b\in\{0,1\}\) and fresh random bits \(r\). It computes a commitment \(C=\mathrm{Commit}(b;r)\) and sends \(C\) to the verifier. Later, it can reveal \((b,r)\), allowing the verifier to recompute the commitment and check that the opening matches.

\[C=\mathrm{Commit}(b;r)\]

Here \(b\) is the secret bit; \(r\) is randomness used while committing; and \(C\) is the public commitment. The semicolon emphasizes that the randomness is a separate input.

Hiding

Before opening, a computationally bounded verifier cannot efficiently tell whether \(C\) contains 0 or 1. The contents remain secret.

Binding

After the prover sends \(C\), it cannot open the same commitment as both 0 and 1. With perfect binding, no alternative opening exists, even for an infinitely powerful prover.

These guarantees do different jobs. Binding prevents a prover from changing its claimed graph after seeing the verifier's challenge; this supports soundness. Hiding prevents the verifier from learning the committed graph before it chooses its challenge; this supports zero knowledge and the simulator's efficiency.

From one bit to a graph

Represent a graph by a matrix \(\boldsymbol{B}\). For distinct vertex labels \(i,j\), the entry \(B_{ij}\) is 1 if the vertices are joined by an edge and 0 otherwise. The prover can commit separately to every entry \(B_{ij}\), while also committing to a secret relabeling of the vertices.

Without knowing the committed bits, the verifier sees sealed evidence whose contents have already been fixed. It can later ask the prover to open either the entire graph or just particular edges. We will exploit these two different openings.

Our next obstacle is subtle: if we always committed to the original graph, revealing it would reveal which edges are which. Instead, we first randomly rename the vertices while preserving every structural property the verifier should be able to test.
