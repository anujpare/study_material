Homework: why the polynomial root bound needs a field

In the later SNARG material we'll use the fact that a nonzero polynomial of degree (d) has at most (d) roots. But there's a condition we must not forget: the numbers must form a field.

Homework 3 · Problem 1(c)

True or false?

The homework claims that \(f(X)=2X^2+4X\) has at most two roots in \(\mathbb Z_6\). Here we calculate modulo 6, keeping remainders in \(\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}\). A root is a value \(a\) where \(f(a)=0\). Test the six possibilities.

The roots are 0, 1, 3, and 4: four distinct roots of a degree-two polynomial. So the statement is false. Why didn't the familiar root bound protect us?

Why the field assumption matters

A field has a multiplicative inverse for every nonzero element. That's what lets us cancel a factor. The ordinary proof of the root bound repeatedly removes \((X-a)\) whenever \(a\) is a root and uses cancellation to keep the other roots in the remaining polynomial.

But modulo 6 we have \(2\cdot3\equiv0\pmod6\), even though neither factor is zero. We cannot cancel nonzero factors as though every one had an inverse. So \(\mathbb Z_6\) isn't a field, and the theorem doesn't apply. The four direct roots show the failure.

This matters later because the soundness of a randomized polynomial test is based on counting how many random choices can be roots of a fixed nonzero polynomial. First verify that you're working over a field.
