Why a polynomial can have more roots than its degree outside a field

This homework question belongs to the polynomial-proof material in the following SNARKs chapter, not the ZK lecture itself. It is included here as a self-contained optional exercise so no HW3 subpart is silently omitted.

Homework 3 · Problem 1(c)

True or false?

“The polynomial \(f(X)=2X^2+4X\) has at most two roots in \(\mathbb Z_6\).” A root is a value \(a\) for which \(f(a)=0\). Here \(\mathbb Z_6\) means we compute results modulo 6, keeping their remainder in \(\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}\).

Check all six possible inputs

\(X\)\(2X^2+4X\)Remainder mod 6Root?
000Yes
160Yes
2164No
3300Yes
4480Yes
5704No

We found four roots: 0, 1, 3, 4, even though the polynomial has degree 2. So the claim is false. The interesting question is which assumption of the usual degree-bound theorem was missing.

Why the field assumption matters

A field is a number system in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. This lets us divide by nonzero quantities. The standard theorem that a nonzero polynomial of degree \(d\) has at most \(d\) roots requires a field. Its proof repeatedly factors out \((X-a)\) after finding a root and relies on the rule that a product is zero only when at least one factor is zero.

Modulo 6, that latter rule fails: \(2\cdot3=6\equiv0\pmod6\), even though both factors are nonzero. These are zero divisors. Because \(\mathbb Z_6\) is not a field, we cannot apply the field root bound. The four direct evaluations above demonstrate the failure.

Root bounds become useful when soundness is reduced to the probability that a randomly sampled input happens to be a root of a nonzero polynomial. That is a later SNARK topic; it was not needed for the Hamiltonicity, Sigma, or Schnorr proofs in the ZK lecture.
