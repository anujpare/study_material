Part VII · Schnorr identification

Turn the Sigma pattern into algebra: Schnorr's protocol

Hamiltonicity used graphs. Schnorr uses multiplication and exponents. We will build the necessary algebra using a tiny numerical example, then prove—without skipping a single equality—why the verifier accepts an honest prover.

The public claim and the secret exponent

Consider a finite set of nonzero numbers closed under multiplication modulo a prime \(p\). “Modulo \(p\)” means we take the remainder after division by \(p\). Choose an element \(g\) whose powers generate a subgroup \(\mathbb G\) of prime order \(q\): there are exactly \(q\) distinct powers, and \(g^q=1\) within the group.

The prover privately holds \(x\in\mathbb Z_q\), where \(\mathbb Z_q=\{0,1,\ldots,q-1\}\) with arithmetic modulo \(q\). It publishes \(h=g^x\) and wants to prove it knows the exponent \(x\) without revealing \(x\).

Why require knowledge here, rather than ordinary soundness? Because \(g\) generates the whole group, every valid public \(h\in\mathbb G\) equals \(g^x\) for some \(x\). The statement “such an \(x\) exists” is therefore automatically true. A sound proof of that statement alone would not authenticate the client; a proof of knowledge must support extraction of \(x\) from a sufficiently successful prover.

\[h=g^x,\qquad u=g^r,\qquad z=r+cx\pmod q\]

The prover chooses a fresh random \(r\in\mathbb Z_q\), sends \(u\); the verifier chooses a challenge \(c\in\mathbb Z_q\); and the prover returns \(z\). The secret is \(x\), while \(g,q,h,u,c,z\) are public or transmitted.

ProverSamples \(r\) uniformly and sends \(u=g^r\).
VerifierSamples \(c\) uniformly from \(\mathbb Z_q\) and sends it.
ProverReturns \(z=(r+cx)\bmod q\). The verifier checks \(g^z=u\,h^c\).

A fully computed example

Use the multiplicative subgroup of integers modulo \(p=23\) generated by \(g=2\). Since \(2^{11}\bmod23=1\), and 11 is prime with \(2\ne1\), this generator has order \(q=11\). So exponents are reduced modulo 11, while the verifier's group multiplications are reduced modulo 23.

Homework 2 · Problem 3(a)

Prove perfect completeness

Given \(h=g^x\), \(u=g^r\) and \(z=r+cx\pmod q\), show that the honest Schnorr prover always satisfies \(g^z=u\,h^c\).

Derive every equality

Because \(z=r+cx\pmod q\) and \(g\) has order \(q\), replacing the exponent by its residue modulo \(q\) does not change the group element. Exponent laws then give:

\[\begin{aligned}g^z&=g^{r+cx}\\&=g^r\,g^{cx}\\&=g^r\,(g^x)^c\\&=u\,h^c.\end{aligned}\]

Line 2 uses \(g^{a+b}=g^a g^b\); line 3 uses \(g^{ab}=(g^a)^b\); line 4 substitutes the definitions of \(u\) and \(h\). So the verifier's equation holds for every honest choice of \(r,c,x\), and completeness probability is 1.

Completeness does not hide the exponent. We must now construct a simulator that produces the same observable \((u,c,z)\) without knowing \(x\). The trick is to choose the response first and solve for the commitment.
