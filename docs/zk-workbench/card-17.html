Part VII · Schnorr identification

Schnorr: prove knowledge of a secret exponent

The Sigma-protocol definition tells us which guarantees to prove, but we still need a concrete example where the extraction equations can be calculated explicitly. Schnorr supplies one: the public statement is a group element, and the private witness is its discrete logarithm. The Hamiltonicity protocol used an adjacency relation, a random permutation of vertices, and a verifier challenge. Schnorr proves knowledge of a discrete logarithm instead, using a different algebraic relation but the same three-message pattern: commitment, challenge, response. We'll verify its equation step by step.

The public claim and the secret exponent

Work with multiplication modulo a prime \(p\): multiply ordinary integers and keep the remainder on division by \(p\). Choose a generator \(g\) whose powers form a cyclic group \(G\) of prime order \(q\). It has \(q\) distinct elements, and \(g^q=1_G\).

Notation warning: In the earlier lecture, \(x\) meant the public statement. In the Schnorr protocol the lecture uses \(x\) for the secret exponent. The public value is now \(h\). We keep the lecture's notation, but these are different roles.

The prover knows a secret exponent \(x\in\mathbb Z_q\), where \(\mathbb Z_q=\{0,\ldots,q-1\}\) with arithmetic modulo \(q\). The public value is \(h=g^x\). The goal is to convince the verifier that the prover knows \(x\), without revealing it.

Why ask for knowledge rather than ordinary soundness? Every element \(h\) of this cyclic group has some exponent \(x\), because \(g\) generates the whole group. So the claim “an \(x\) exists” is always true for a valid \(h\). Authentication must tell us that this prover knows that exponent.

\[h=g^x,\qquad u=g^r,\qquad z=r+cx\pmod q\]

The prover chooses fresh random \(r\), sends \(u=g^r\), receives random challenge \(c\), and answers \(z=r+cx\pmod q\). The prover keeps \(x,r\) secret. The verifier knows \(g,q,h,u,c,z\).

A fully computed example

To see the arithmetic, let \(p=23\) and \(g=2\). Since \(2^{11}\equiv1\pmod{23}\), \(2\ne1\), and 11 is prime, \(g\) has order \(q=11\). We reduce exponents modulo 11 and group products modulo 23.

Homework 2 · Problem 3(a)

Prove perfect completeness

Given \(h=g^x\), \(u=g^r\), and \(z=r+cx\pmod q\), why must \(g^z=u h^c\) hold for every honest prover?

Derive every equality

Because \(g\) has order \(q\), reducing \(z\) modulo \(q\) doesn't change \(g^z\). Use the usual exponent laws to expand \(g^{r+cx}\):

\[\begin{aligned}g^z&=g^{r+cx}\\&=g^r\,g^{cx}\\&=g^r\,(g^x)^c\\&=u\,h^c.\end{aligned}\]

The derivation uses \(g^{a+b}=g^ag^b\), then \(g^{ab}=(g^a)^b\), then substitutes \(g^r=u\) and \(g^x=h\). So the two sides of the verifier's test are equal for every honest \(r,c,x\), which proves perfect completeness.

Passing the verifier's equation does not itself prove privacy. We must show a simulator can create the same observable transcript without the secret exponent. Oddly enough, it will do so by choosing the response first.
