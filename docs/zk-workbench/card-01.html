What is a proof actually proving?

A zero-knowledge proof sounds mysterious only if we skip the ordinary idea of a proof. Start by separating the claim from the evidence that convinces someone it is true.

A claim about five cities

Imagine that the lines below are roads. I claim that you can start at city A, visit each of the five cities exactly once, and return to A using only these roads. The claim is either true or false, whether or not I tell you a route.

Finding evidence versus checking evidence

Finding such a route may require trying many possibilities. Checking a proposed route is much simpler: look at every consecutive pair of cities, confirm that a road joins them, and check that each city appears once before returning to the start.

We call the claim the statement, written x, and a proposed route the witness, written w. A verifier is an algorithm that checks whether the witness establishes the claim. It accepts by returning 1 and rejects by returning 0.

Where NP enters

In this course, NP means a class of yes/no problems for which, whenever the answer is yes, there exists a witness of manageable length that a computer can check efficiently. “Efficiently” means the checking time grows at most as a polynomial in the size of the input, rather than requiring an exhaustive search of every possible witness.

x ∈ L ⇔ there exists w with V(x, w) = 1L is the set of yes-instances; ∈ means “belongs to”; ⇔ means “if and only if”. The witness is short enough to check efficiently.

For our map, the witness is one valid round trip. The important distinction is that being able to verify evidence does not mean you know how to find it quickly.

What remains unresolved? This ordinary proof sends the full route to the verifier, who can therefore learn it. On the next card, we introduce interaction—the first step toward convincing a verifier without simply handing over the witness.
