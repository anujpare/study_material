Part I · What counts as a proof?

What would convince us that a graph has a cycle?

Suppose I tell you there is a route through five cities that visits every city exactly once and comes back to the start. You needn't trust me. I can show you the route, and you can check it yourself.

A precise question

Look at the map. The cities are numbered 1 through 5, and a line means there is a road. Our claim is that we can visit all five cities exactly once and return to the start without ever using a missing road. Such a route is called a Hamiltonian cycle.

Gray lines are available roads. Choosing a candidate below marks each claimed road green if it exists, or dashed red if it does not.

Explore the crucial distinction

Can the verifier check this proposed proof?

The graph itself is unchanged. Only the proposed route changes.

Choose one route. The verifier will inspect every road rather than trusting the claim.

Try both routes. Route A shows that the claim is true: every required road exists and every city is visited once. Route B fails because one of its roads is missing. But that only proves Route B is invalid; it does not prove the graph has no valid route. One successful witness settles a YES claim, while one failed witness cannot settle a NO claim.

From a road map to mathematical language

Let's name the pieces. The statement \(x\) is the graph. The witness \(w\) is the proposed route. The checking algorithm \(M\) takes both and returns 1 if the route is valid, or 0 if it is not. Notice that the verifier never has to know how the route was found.

\[M(x,w)=1\]

The value 1 means that the checker accepts the witness. The value 0 means that this particular witness fails. The equation does not claim that every possible witness succeeds.

We've learned something useful: a proof can be evidence that the verifier checks, rather than a description of how the prover solved the problem. We will soon want to convince the verifier without handing over the witness.

Checking the supplied route is quick. But how much work would it take to find a route when nobody gives us one? That's the distinction behind NP.
