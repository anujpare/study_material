Part I · What counts as a proof?

A claim is not the same thing as its evidence

We want to prove a statement without revealing a secret. Before asking how that could work, we must understand what an ordinary proof reveals. A small road-map problem lets us separate the claim, the evidence and the act of checking the evidence.

A precise question

Consider a map with five cities numbered 1 to 5. A line means that a road connects two cities. Our claim is: there exists a route that visits every city exactly once and returns to its starting city, travelling only along the drawn roads. Such a route is called a Hamiltonian cycle.

Gray lines are available roads. Choosing a candidate below marks each claimed road green if it exists, or dashed red if it does not.

Explore the crucial distinction

Can the verifier check this proposed proof?

The graph itself is unchanged. Only the proposed route changes.

Choose one route. The verifier will inspect every road rather than trusting the claim.

Even if Route B fails verification, that does not establish that the graph has no Hamiltonian cycle: it establishes only that this proposed route is not valid. Route A, however, is enough to establish that the original claim is true because it exhibits a valid cycle.

From a road map to mathematical language

We call the claim's input the statement, denoted \(x\). Here \(x\) is the complete road map. We call a proposed piece of evidence a witness, denoted \(w\). Here \(w\) is the ordered list of visited cities. Finally, \(M\) is the algorithm that checks whether the witness satisfies the statement.

\[M(x,w)=1\]

The value 1 means that the checker accepts the witness. The value 0 means that this particular witness fails. The equation does not claim that every possible witness succeeds.

A proof need not explain how the prover found the route. It only needs to supply evidence that the verifier can check. This difference becomes important when we study complexity, and later when we ask how to hide the witness itself.

Why the next card? The verifier checks one proposed route quickly. Does that imply that finding such a route—or determining that none exists—is equally quick? Answering that requires a precise definition of NP.
