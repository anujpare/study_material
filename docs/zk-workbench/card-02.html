02 · Interactive proofs

Why does the verifier ask questions?

In the previous card, the prover sent a complete route and the verifier checked it. Now imagine that the prover wants to persuade the verifier without simply revealing all the evidence. Interaction gives the verifier another tool: an unpredictable question.

Who does what?

The prover, P, tries to establish that a statement x is true. The verifier, V, checks the prover’s messages and finally accepts or rejects. Unlike a one-shot proof, an interactive proof can contain several messages in both directions. The verifier may use random choices that the prover cannot predict in advance.

1
Prover preparesSends an initial message before seeing the challenge.
2
Verifier challengesChooses a random question, for example the bit 0 or 1.
3
Prover responds; verifier checksThe verifier decides whether this particular answer is acceptable.

Here a bit is a value that can be 0 or 1. It is the verifier that chooses the challenge—not the prover. This order matters: if the question were announced first, a dishonest prover could prepare only the answer it knows will be requested.

The two guarantees we need

Completeness protects an honest prover with a true statement: following the prescribed protocol should make the verifier accept with sufficiently high probability. Soundness protects the verifier against a dishonest prover attempting to establish a false statement.

Completeness · true xPr[accept] ≥ 2/3When P is honest and x is true, the honest verifier accepts at least two times out of three on average.
Soundness · false xPr[accept] ≤ 1/3For every cheating strategy P*, if x is false, the honest verifier accepts no more than one time out of three on average.

Pr means probability—the chance of an event. The fractions 2/3 and 1/3 are conventional thresholds in the lecture definition, not magical constants. Repeating a suitable protocol can make an error probability much smaller.

Can a cheater predict the question?

Consider a toy situation, not yet a real zero-knowledge protocol. Assume a false claim means the dishonest prover can prepare a valid answer for only one of two checks. It has prepared for challenge 0. The verifier chooses a fresh, fair random challenge after that preparation is locked.

Try a round: 0 means the cheater got lucky, whereas 1 exposes the false claim.

Pr[cheat succeeds in 4 rounds] = (1/2)⁴ = 1/16This calculation assumes each fresh challenge is independent and the cheater can answer at most one of the two checks in each round.

Does interaction already mean zero knowledge?

No. An interactive protocol might still reveal the secret witness in its replies. So far we have defined only the mechanics of an interactive proof and the two checks on its correctness. Zero knowledge adds a third requirement: even a malicious verifier must not learn the secret witness from participating.

The next conceptual step: We will formalize “learns nothing” by asking whether the verifier could reproduce an indistinguishable conversation without possessing the witness at all. That is the idea behind a simulator.
