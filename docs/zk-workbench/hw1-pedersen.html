Homework 1 · Commitment prerequisites · Problems 2(c,d)

Why does knowing the discrete logarithm destroy one form of binding?

Two group-based commitments look similar. Yet if the committer knows the discrete-logarithm relation between their public generators, one loses binding completely while the other remains binding but loses information-theoretic privacy.

Pedersen commitments with a known generator relation

Let \(\mathbb G\) be a cyclic group of prime order \(q\) with generator \(g\). Choose \(h=g^t\) for a nonzero \(t\in\mathbb Z_q\); the assumption \(t\ne0\) ensures that \(h\) is also a generator. The committer makes \(C=g^m h^r\), where \(m\) is the message and \(r\) is random.

\[C=g^m h^r=g^{m+tr}\]

We use \(t\) for the known relation between \(g\) and \(h\), to avoid confusing it with the Schnorr secret \(x\) introduced later. The equality follows from \(h=g^t\) and exponent laws.

Suppose the committer already sent \(C\), opened honestly as \((m,r)\), and then wants to claim it committed to some different \(m'\). It needs a new salt \(r'\) solving \(m'+tr'\equiv m+tr\pmod q\). Solving gives:

\[r'=r+(m-m')t^{-1}\pmod q\]

The value \(t^{-1}\) is the multiplicative inverse of \(t\) modulo prime \(q\). It exists because \(t\ne0\). Substituting this \(r'\) makes \(g^{m'}h^{r'}=g^m h^r=C\).

HW1 2(c) · Exhibit two different openings

Change the claimed message without changing the commitment

Use \(q=11\), \(p=23\), \(g=2\), and \(h=g^3=8\) modulo 23. The originally committed message is \(m=4\), with salt \(r=2\). Here the committer knows \(t=3\).

Original opening\(m=4,\ r=2\)
Alternative opening\(m'=7,\ r'=1\)
The two sides should always calculate the same fixed group element \(C\).

Show hiding survives even though binding fails

Because \(r\) is uniform modulo \(q\) and \(t\ne0\), the exponent \(m+tr\) is uniform modulo \(q\) for every fixed message \(m\). The map \(r\mapsto m+tr\pmod q\) is a bijection, so the resulting group element \(g^{m+tr}\) is uniform in \(\mathbb G\). Thus the distribution of \(C\) is identical for all messages: the scheme is perfectly hiding. The two alternative openings above establish that it is not binding when the committer knows \(t\).

HW1 2(d) · Why the ElGamal-style pair behaves differently

Now the commitment is an ordered pair rather than one group element:

\[C=(g^r,\ g^m h^r)\]

Again \(h=g^t\) and the relation \(t\) is assumed known. The verifier receives both components, not merely \(g^m h^r\).

Prove binding

If two openings \((m,r)\) and \((m',r')\) yield the same pair, equality of the first components gives \(g^r=g^{r'}\). Since \(g\) has order \(q\), \(r=r'\pmod q\). Equality of the second components then gives \(g^m h^r=g^{m'}h^{r'}\), so the matching \(h\) factors cancel, leaving \(g^m=g^{m'}\). Hence \(m=m'\pmod q\). Two different messages cannot open the same commitment: it is perfectly binding.

Disprove statistical hiding

Because \(t\) is known, the observer divides the second public component by the first raised to \(t\):

\[\frac{g^m h^r}{(g^r)^t}=\frac{g^m g^{tr}}{g^{tr}}=g^m.\]

An unbounded observer can calculate the discrete logarithm \(m\) of this result, so the message is recoverable: the scheme is not statistically hiding. If the message domain is small, even an efficient observer may recover \(m\) by enumeration.

We have now proved what hiding and binding mean in concrete algebra. Return to the main ZK construction: the prover can use a properly chosen binding-and-hiding commitment scheme to fix a graph while keeping its entries secret.
