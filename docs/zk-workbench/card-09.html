Part III · Graph Hamiltonicity

Prove the graph protocol's two guarantees

We know the two questions the verifier may ask. Let's prove exactly what they guarantee. A real cycle must pass either question. If \(G\) has no cycle, a cheater with fixed commitments cannot answer both.

Proof 1 · Perfect completeness

For completeness, assume \(G\) has a Hamiltonian cycle \(w\). The honest prover chooses a random permutation \(\Pi\) and commits to \(B_{ij}=1\iff\{\Pi(i),\Pi(j)\}\in E\).

If \(c=0\), it opens the full graph and the permutation. By construction every edge agrees with \(B_{ij}=1\iff\{\Pi(i),\Pi(j)\}\in E\), so the verifier accepts.

If \(c=1\), it takes its known cycle \(w\), renames every vertex using \(\Pi^{-1}\), and reveals the corresponding edges of \(B\). A permutation preserves edge membership and visits each vertex exactly once, so the verifier accepts.

Those are the only possible challenges. An honest prover therefore passes with probability \(1\). That proves perfect completeness.

Soundness starts with the assumption that the claim is false

Now assume the claim is false: \(G\) has no Hamiltonian cycle. The cheating prover must commit to a graph \(B\) before the verifier picks \(c\). Perfect binding is needed here: without it, the prover could change \(B\) after seeing the question.

There are only two possibilities for the graph the cheater fixed:

Case A

Case A. \(B\) really is a relabeling of \(G\). Then \(B\) cannot contain a Hamiltonian cycle, because renaming preserves whether a cycle exists. Challenge \(c=1\) must therefore fail.

Case B

Case B. \(B\) is not a relabeling of \(G\). Challenge \(c=0\) must fail, because opening everything would reveal a graph that does not match the public one.

Proof 2 · Soundness error at most \(1/2\)

We've covered every possible committed graph: either it is a relabeling of \(G\) or it is not. In the first case \(c=1\) fails; in the second case \(c=0\) fails. Since the verifier chooses each challenge with probability \(1/2\), the cheater can succeed with probability at most \(1/2\).

\[\Pr[\mathrm{accept}\mid G\notin L]\ \leq\ \tfrac12\]

That is the soundness proof. It isn't a guess about how an ordinary cheater behaves. It shows that for every fixed cheating commitment, at least one of the two questions is impossible to answer correctly.

Only binding was needed for this soundness argument. Hiding serves a different purpose: it will let a simulator imitate what the verifier sees without knowing the secret cycle.

Now comes the harder question: can we produce a convincing-looking conversation without knowing any cycle? That will be our zero-knowledge proof.
