Part IV · Proving zero knowledge

How do we simulate a verifier's view without a witness?

The graph protocol has shown how a verifier can be convinced. But conviction alone does not establish zero knowledge: the conversation might still reveal something about the hidden cycle. We need a way to test whether the verifier learned anything beyond the truth of the claim. The standard test is to construct a simulator \(\mathrm{Sim}\) that produces an indistinguishable verifier view without knowing the cycle. If it can do that efficiently, the view itself cannot serve as evidence that the verifier learned the witness. The next construction shows how to simulate each possible challenge.

Construct a response to one guessed challenge

The trick is to guess the question before preparing the commitments. The simulator picks \(\hat c\in\{0,1\}\) at random, and makes a first message it can open correctly if the verifier asks \(\hat c\).

Guess \(\hat c=0\)

Preparing for \(c=0\): choose a random permutation \(\Pi\), build the real relabeled graph \(B_{ij}=1\iff\{\Pi(i),\Pi(j)\}\in E\), and commit to its entries. If asked to reveal the whole graph and renaming, the simulator can do that. It never needed the secret cycle.

Guess \(\hat c=1\)

Preparing for \(c=1\): commit to a complete graph, which contains an edge between every pair of distinct vertices, and select a uniformly relabeled Hamiltonian cycle to reveal. A complete graph certainly has a Hamiltonian cycle, so the simulator can show one without finding any cycle in \(G\).

But the complete graph generally isn't a relabeling of \(G\). It could fail \(c=0\). And the genuine relabeling may be impossible to open as a cycle without the witness. That's why the simulator prepares for just one guessed challenge.

Commitments are not sent until the simulator chooses a preparation.

Each concealed matrix entry represents a committed adjacency bit. The verifier sees its value only when the simulator opens that commitment in response to the guessed challenge. A new simulation attempt uses fresh randomness.

The simulator must send its first message before receiving \(c\).

If the verifier asks \(c=\hat c\), the simulator opens its commitments and keeps the resulting conversation. If \(c\ne\hat c\), it discards the attempt and runs the verifier again from the state saved before that first message, with new randomness. This is called rewinding. It is a power of the simulated experiment, not something the real prover is allowed to do after sending messages.

\[\hat c\ \xleftarrow{\$}\ \{0,1\},\qquad \text{keep attempt only if }\hat c=c\]

The symbol \(\hat c\) means the simulator's guess. It is chosen before the verifier's real challenge \(c\) becomes known.

We have a method for producing accepting transcripts without the witness. But acceptance alone does not establish zero knowledge. We must prove that the simulated verifier view is indistinguishable from the real view and that the simulator runs efficiently.

Those are the two questions we'll answer next.
