Lecture §3 · Extraction by rewinding

Recover a Hamiltonian cycle from two accepted answers

Recall the Hamiltonicity protocol. The prover commits to a randomly permuted graph \(B\) and a vertex permutation \(\Pi\). Challenge 0 asks it to open the graph and permutation, proving that \(B\) is a relabeling of the public graph \(G\). Challenge 1 asks it to reveal a Hamiltonian cycle in \(B\) while keeping the permutation hidden.

Each answer alone can be produced without exposing the original cycle. But an extractor can rewind the prover: it saves the prover's complete state immediately after the first commitments, asks challenge 0, restores that exact state, then asks challenge 1. Because the first commitments are identical in both runs, the two responses concern the same graph \(B\).

Combine two accepting transcripts

Which information becomes extractable after rewinding?

One response alone doesn't reveal the secret: the full graph opening gives the permutation but not necessarily a cycle; the cycle opening gives a cycle of the renamed graph but hides the renaming. The extractor obtains both.

First identify what each transcript reveals. Then show how the two transcripts reconstruct the original witness.

Why the two responses reveal a valid witness

The accepted response to challenge 0 gives the permutation \(\Pi\) and proves that \(B\) has the edges of the original graph \(G\) after relabeling. The accepted response to challenge 1 gives a cycle \((b_1,\ldots,b_n,b_1)\) in \(B\) that visits each vertex exactly once.

Apply the permutation from challenge 0 to the vertices of this cycle. Because a permutation is one-to-one, no two distinct cycle vertices become the same original vertex. Because the graph opening established the correspondence of edges, every consecutive pair becomes an actual edge of \(G\). The mapped cycle therefore visits every vertex of \(G\) exactly once: it is a valid Hamiltonian-cycle witness.

The lecturer proves this extraction idea informally for a prover accepted with probability 1. A full proof of knowledge for arbitrary acceptance probabilities must additionally analyze the probability and running time of rewinding; that full analysis is not supplied in the notes. \(\square\)

Two different uses of rewinding

A zero-knowledge simulator rewinds the verifier to produce a believable view without knowing the witness. A knowledge extractor rewinds the prover to reconstruct a genuine witness. These are different security experiments, so extraction does not mean that the ordinary verifier can learn the secret.

Homework 3 · Problem 1(a)

Can a small knowledge error coexist with a larger soundness error?

A cheating prover convinces the verifier of a false statement with probability \(p>1/3\). Could the protocol still be a proof of knowledge with error \(\varepsilon<1/3\)?

Derive the contradiction

Because the statement is false, no valid witness exists, so the extractor succeeds with probability 0. Definition 4 would require \(0\ge p-\varepsilon\). Hence \(p\le\varepsilon\), contradicting \(p>1/3\) and \(\varepsilon<1/3\).

We used a simple and important pattern: one fixed first message, two different challenges, and two accepting responses reveal a witness. Sigma protocols make that extraction condition a formal property called special soundness.
