# PETS teaching and authoring guide

This is an editable agreement about how PETS teaches. Read it before changing a deck. The learner is encountering this course for the first time, has not attended the lectures or solved the exercises, and is preparing for an exam. Teach the course's actual technical language through clear, connected explanations and cryptographic examples. This is a design framework, not a claim that one format works for every learner.

**Priority:** explicit current user instructions, then the learner preferences below, then general research-informed guidance. Do not turn a preference into a reason to omit necessary reasoning. The existing HTML establishes the reader's dimensions and navigation.

## 1. Prerequisite sequencing and scaffolding

- **S1 — First exposure means first exposure. [Learner preference]** Do not assume the learner has read the notes or remembers an earlier definition. Identify the knowledge needed to understand the next claim, including notation. A Hamiltonian cycle must be defined before a proof about one; a field must be explained before a polynomial root bound.
- **S2 — Build a dependency chain. [Authoring rule]** Record what each card teaches and what it requires. Move an explanation before its first use. Use half a card or a full prerequisite card when necessary, instead of inserting an unexplained term into a later solution.
- **S3 — Give a useful mental map. [Learner preference]** At the start of a construction, identify the goal, the objects being built, and how they will fit together. Give each unfamiliar item a brief meaning in that map; a list of unexplained names is not a map.
- **S4 — Introduce notation locally. [Learner preference]** Say what the symbols represent, their domains, and how to read the expression. Distinguish a polynomial's variable X from an evaluation point r, a statement x from a secret exponent, and an index from the value stored there. Explain a change of meaning rather than relying on the same letter.
- **S5 — Use pre-training and manageable segments. [Research-informed]** Introduce the names and roles of key components before combining them. Let the learner control the pace of a multi-step visual. These are the *pre-training* and *segmenting* principles; they support a dependency-first sequence, not a universal card-length limit. See [Mayer and Fiorella, managing essential processing](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108894333.025).
- **S6 — Place homework just in time. [Learner preference]** Insert an actual homework subquestion immediately after its final required concept. Keep a separate homework-only deck, generated from the same question and solution. Preserve the assignment's identifiers; do not invent questions and label them as assigned homework.
- **S7 — Budget cards by conceptual need. [Learner preference]** Avoid extra cards that merely restate earlier ones. Do not meet a small card budget by skipping a prerequisite, an inference, or a necessary assumption. Keep existing card URLs stable when reorganizing material.

## 2. Explanatory coherence and explicit reasoning

- **E1 — Explain once, clearly. [Learner preference]** Use complete, natural sentences in the manner of a careful textbook. Avoid compressed fragments, redundant captions, repeated summaries and several paraphrases of the same fact. A short explanation that omits its reason is not an improvement.
- **E2 — Supply the inferential link. [Authoring rule]** For each consequential step, explain: what we know, the operation or theorem we use, why its assumptions hold, and what follows. For example, do not divide modulo q until the denominator is shown to have an inverse.
- **E3 — Stay in the subject. [Learner preference]** Prefer concrete examples involving commitments, proof transcripts, verifier checks, circuit values or attacker queries. Do not substitute distant metaphors for technical understanding. Introduce the correct course term and make it intelligible.
- **E4 — Use one running example where it helps. [Authoring rule]** Carry the same values through a circuit, its trace, its polynomial encoding and its checks. State when arithmetic changes from integers to a finite field. A small numerical example illustrates an argument; it does not prove a general security theorem.
- **E5 — Separate guarantees and models. [Authoring rule]** Distinguish completeness, soundness, knowledge extraction, privacy and succinctness. Say which party follows the protocol, which attacks are allowed, and whether the model is plain, CRS, random-oracle or ideal-oracle access. Three messages alone do not establish the properties of a Sigma protocol.
- **E6 — Be faithful without reproducing errors. [Authoring rule]** Check calculations and identify omissions in supplied solutions. Correct them in the teaching text and record the source issue in `SOURCE_NOTES.md`. Do not silently turn a heuristic, an informal sketch or an ambiguous exercise into an unconditional theorem.
- **E7 — Keep useful information, remove distraction. [Research-informed]** *Coherence* concerns excluding irrelevant material; *signaling* concerns marking the structure of relevant material. Neither means making prose terse. The multimedia *redundancy* principle has a specific narrated-media setting; it is not evidence that all recap or repeated practice is harmful. See [Mayer and Fiorella, reducing extraneous processing](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning/F29A19FCD34C542806F736E0661C05F5).

## 3. Visual representations and purposeful interaction

- **V1 — Give every visual a teaching job. [Learner preference]** Choose a diagram for relationships or message order, a table for cases or values, a plot for quantitative change, and an interaction for a counterfactual the learner can inspect. Decorative motion is not a teaching objective.
- **V2 — Integrate words and representations. [Research-informed]** Put an explanation beside the equation, node or plotted quantity it explains. Show the same objects and symbols across prose and diagram. This applies *spatial contiguity* and the recommendation to connect concrete and abstract representations. See the [IES practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1).
- **V3 — Make consequences visible. [Authoring rule]** A control should answer a question such as: Which equality fails if this wire copy changes? How many challenge vectors can a false proof answer? Show the changed values, the verifier's decision and the reason for it.
- **V4 — Reveal a derivation at the learner's pace. [Learner preference]** Use explicit previous/next steps when a whole proof would be overwhelming. Keep earlier values available. Avoid autoplay, hidden assumptions and controls that change several unrelated concepts at once.
- **V5 — Respect the mathematics. [Authoring rule]** Label axes, units, sets and probabilities. Finite-field evaluations are discrete points, not an ordinary smooth real curve. A bound is not an observed success rate. A sampled numerical check is not a proof for all inputs. Authentication of a value is separate from satisfaction of a logical constraint.
- **V6 — Keep the page coherent. [Learner preference]** Integrate diagrams into the explanation. On existing dense cards, an Explore/Read choice can provide two representations without stacking both. Keep equations and the full written reasoning available. A visual must not become a replacement for an omitted explanation.
- **V7 — Preserve the interface contract. [Learner preference]** Public branding is PETS. Use the subtle three-line chapter popup, the existing single-card reader, and its navigation. There are only two text sizes: the current standard card text and exactly 2 pt smaller for boxes and diagrams. Use weight, spacing and structure for emphasis, not additional sizes.

## 4. Practice, retrieval, transfer and feedback

- **P1 — Make the exercise attempt visible. [Learner preference]** Show the question before its solution. Keep solutions closed initially. Give the learner a chance to retrieve or derive the next step rather than displaying every answer in an introductory caption.
- **P2 — Write worked solutions that can teach. [Learner preference]** Recap the needed definition where it is used, then work through the reasoning. Define local symbols, justify transformations and explain the conclusion. Homework-only cards must be usable without having another deck open.
- **P3 — Connect explanation and practice. [Research-informed]** Alternate worked examples with problems, ask why a step follows, and use retrieval with feedback. The IES guide supports these methods with differing evidence ratings; it does not prescribe this exact interface. See [its recommendations and evidence levels](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1).
- **P4 — Distinguish repetition from retrieval. [Learner preference + application]** Remove repeated exposition. Retain purposeful practice and brief reminders needed to solve an exercise. Optional delayed revisiting of a question can support retention; do not force the learner through a duplicate lecture to achieve spacing.
- **P5 — Make feedback explanatory. [Authoring rule]** Explain which assumption or equality makes an answer correct. For a wrong answer, identify the specific misconception and give a route to correct it. Show the explanation once, rather than duplicating it in both an output panel and a feedback box.
- **P6 — Preserve exam fidelity. [Learner preference]** Use the lecture's terminology and the actual homework's scope. Distinguish an exam-length conclusion from the worked reasoning needed to understand it. An assignment's “one sentence” instruction does not require the teaching solution to omit its derivation.

## 5. Evaluation, accessibility and continuous improvement

- **Q1 — Review content before polishing. [Authoring rule]** Check dependency order, coverage of the supplied notes, homework placement, symbol definitions, every numerical example, and the assumptions of each probability bound. Record source pages and known scope limits in the content map.
- **Q2 — Test the experience. [Authoring rule]** Render every card and expanded solution, check math errors and clipping, and test navigation, deep links, menus, keyboard/touch controls, both text sizes and small screens. Check the original reading path without JavaScript. Report browser/device coverage accurately.
- **Q3 — Keep explanations maintainable. [Authoring rule]** Generate chapter/homework copies from one source. Keep this guide and source corrections on GitHub with reviewable changes. Do not let a generator reintroduce a removed paragraph or a misleading diagram.
- **Q4 — Diagnose feedback, then change the rule or its enforcement. [Learner preference]** When the learner identifies a failure, locate the relevant bullet and classify it as **missing guidance**, **guidance ignored**, or **guidance too weak/ambiguous**. Add or strengthen the relevant bullet only when needed; otherwise fix the implementation and its review check. Record a concrete example and corrective action below. Never treat the learner's confusion as lack of effort.
- **Q5 — Do not promise what was not checked. [Authoring rule]** Keep source coverage, mathematical verification, layout tests and teaching judgment separate. Passing a browser test cannot establish that a proof is correct or that every learner understands it.
